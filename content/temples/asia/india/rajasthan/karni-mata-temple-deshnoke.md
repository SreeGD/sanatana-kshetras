Karni Mata, a 15th-16th century mystic of the Charan community, is venerated here not as a goddess descended from myth but as a real woman whose life is remembered as an embodiment of Shakti; the shrine at Deshnoke is said to have been founded around 1530, shortly after her mahaprayan, on the site associated with her life. The temple's present form — a white marble facade with heavy silver doors — dates only to the early twentieth century, a rebuilding commissioned by Maharaja Ganga Singh of Bikaner in Rajput architectural style; almost nothing of the earlier structure is described in accessible sources.

What sets Deshnoke apart is the roughly 20,000 rats that live within the temple courtyards and are fed, protected, and revered rather than driven out. According to the temple's own tradition, when a young relative of Karni Mata drowned, she persuaded Yamraj, lord of death, to let the souls of her family's men be reborn as rats instead of returning to the ordinary cycle of rebirth; a handful of white rats among the thousands are believed to be Karni Mata and her four nephews in that form, and glimpsing one is considered especially auspicious. Sharing food nibbled by the rats is treated as receiving the goddess's blessing, and a pilgrim who accidentally kills one is traditionally expected to replace it with an offering of silver or gold. The site draws its largest crowds during the two annual Navratri fairs, and has drawn wider attention through documentaries and travel journalism curious about a temple built around the deliberate protection of vermin.
