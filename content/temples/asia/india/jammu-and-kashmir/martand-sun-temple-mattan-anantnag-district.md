Even in ruin, the Martand Sun Temple is one of the most ambitious works of architecture Kashmir ever produced. It was commissioned in the 8th century CE by Lalitaditya Muktapida, the most expansionist ruler of the Karkota dynasty, as a temple to Surya, the sun god, at Mattan near present-day Anantnag. The design paired a central sanctuary with a vast colonnaded courtyard ringed by 84 smaller shrines, and its carved stonework drew on an unusually wide mix of influences — Gandharan, Gupta, and other regional traditions have all been read into its surviving details — reflecting Kashmir's position as a crossroads of trade and pilgrimage routes in that period.

The temple did not survive Kashmir's later political and religious upheavals: tradition credits its demolition to Sultan Sikandar Shah Miri, whose reputation as a destroyer of temples earned him the regional epithet "Butshikan," idol-breaker, and further centuries of earthquakes compounded the damage to what he left behind. What remains today is a roofless colonnade of grey limestone, protected as a national monument by the Archaeological Survey of India, with restoration work reported to have begun in 2024. No regular worship takes place at the ruin; its significance today is chiefly historical and architectural, as one of the clearest surviving witnesses to Kashmir's pre-Islamic temple-building tradition.
