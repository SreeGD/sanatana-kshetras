## Origin and legend

Sri Padmanabhaswamy Temple, in the heart of Thiruvananthapuram, enshrines Vishnu as Anantha Padmanabha, reclining on the serpent Ananta (Adisesha), with Sridevi (Lakshmi) and Bhudevi at his side and a lotus emerging from his navel bearing Brahma. Temple legend traces the founding to the sage Divakara Muni, an ardent devotee of Vishnu who lived nearby. Vishnu is said to have appeared to the sage as a child, who then transformed into an enormous reclining form stretching from the Cape (Kanyakumari) to Sri Lanka. Overwhelmed, the sage asked the Lord to reduce his form; Vishnu obliged, appearing instead in a form three times the length of the sage's staff (katusarkara yogam, a stucco of a special composite material) -- the image worshipped today. The site itself, "Anantankaadu" (forest of Ananta), is remembered as the original forest shrine before the present structure was raised over it.

## Historical significance

The temple is one of the richest in the world, brought to global attention in 2011 when a Supreme Court-mandated inventory of its sealed vaults revealed vast quantities of gold, jewels, and artifacts accumulated over centuries of royal and devotee offerings. Its documented history is deeply entwined with the Travancore royal family: in 1750, Maharajah Marthanda Varma performed the Thripadidanam, ceremonially gifting the entire kingdom of Travancore to the deity and thereafter ruling as "Padmanabha Dasa" (servant of Padmanabha), a title the royal family retains to this day. The present gopuram and much of the granite corridor architecture reflect renovations and expansions carried out under later Travancore rulers, layered atop a much older core shrine.

## Connection to the Alvars and Divya Prabandham

Thiruvananthapuram is counted among the thirteen Malai Nadu Divya Desams -- the Divya Desams of the old Chera/hill country, today's Kerala. Nammalvar, one of the twelve Vaishnava Alvar saints whose Tamil hymns make up the Nalayira Divya Prabandham, sang verses glorifying the Lord of this shrine in the Thiruvaimozhi, placing Thiruvananthapuram among the temples "seen and sung" by the Alvars between roughly the 6th and 9th centuries CE -- among the very few Divya Desams outside the Tamil country proper. This inclusion anchors the temple firmly within the pan-Tamil Sri Vaishnava devotional canon even though it lies at the traditional geographic edge of that map, on the Arabian Sea coast of the old Chera kingdom.
