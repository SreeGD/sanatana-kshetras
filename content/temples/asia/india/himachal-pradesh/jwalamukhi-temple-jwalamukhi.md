A natural flame burning from a fissure in bare rock, fed by an underground gas seam, has drawn pilgrims to this Kangra hillside for centuries without anyone ever having had to light it. The earliest source clearly naming the shrine is the 14th-century Persian chronicle Tarikh-i-Firuz-Shahi, which records Sultan Firuz Shah Tughlaq visiting it during a campaign into the Kangra hills; two centuries later, Akbar's chronicler Abul Fazl offered his own explanation for the perpetual fire, attributing it to a natural gas or sulfur deposit rather than a miracle. In devotional tradition the temple is one of the Shakti Peethas, the fissure's flame revered as the tongue of the goddess Sati, whose torso is said to have fallen at the linked Vajreshwari Devi temple in nearby Kangra town — a mythic geography that ties the two shrines together in pilgrim practice even though it rests on tradition rather than documentary record. The present sanctum, with its golden dome and a silver-plated door gifted by the Sikh ruler Kharak Singh in the 19th century, reflects centuries of royal patronage layered onto what is, at its core, a site of natural-phenomenon worship.
