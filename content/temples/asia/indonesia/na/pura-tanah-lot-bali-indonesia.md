Pura Tanah Lot ("land in the sea") is one of seven principal sea temples ringing Bali's southwestern coastline in traditional Balinese Hindu geography, perched on a small offshore rock accessible on foot only at low tide. Balinese tradition, recorded in the palm-leaf manuscript Dwijendra Tatwa, credits its founding to the 16th-century wandering Hindu priest Dang Hyang Nirartha, said to have spent a night at the site during his travels across Bali, Lombok, and Sumbawa and to have directed local fishermen to build a shrine there; Nirartha himself is venerated at the temple alongside its principal deity, Dewa Baruna (also called Bhatara Segara), guardian of the sea.

As at other Balinese temples, ritual life follows the 210-day pawukon calendar, with periodic odalan (temple-anniversary) ceremonies, and a freshwater spring at the base of the rock, considered sacred despite its proximity to the sea, is used by pilgrims for ritual purification. The temple's sea-facing sandstone base has required active conservation to prevent erosion, including government-supported restoration work in the 1980s with Japanese assistance. Today Tanah Lot functions simultaneously as a living site of Balinese Hindu worship and as one of Bali's most-visited tourist landmarks, drawing hundreds of thousands of visitors annually, particularly at sunset.
