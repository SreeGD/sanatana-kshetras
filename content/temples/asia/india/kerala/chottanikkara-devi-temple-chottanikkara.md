Local tradition traces Chottanikkara's origin to a tribal hunter named Kannappan who lived in the forest around the temple's present site. As the story is told, his daughter Pavizham was killed trying to stop him from sacrificing a calf; searching the site afterward in grief, Kannappan found a radiant stone slab standing where the sacrificial altar had been, and a priest identified it as a manifestation of the divine couple Vishnu and Lakshmi. That self-manifested (svayambhu) laterite formation, known as the Rudraksha Sila, remains the temple's central object of worship in place of a carved image.

Administered today by the Cochin Devaswom Board, the temple worships its presiding goddess, Rajarajeswari — an aspect of Adiparashakthi — in three different forms across a single day: as Saraswathi in the morning, Lakshmi at midday, and Kali by evening, a daily transformation distinctive among Kerala's Devi temples. Vishnu shares the same pedestal as the goddess, jointly worshipped as Lakshmi Narayana, while subsidiary shrines within the complex honor Shiva, Brahma, Ganapathi, Subrahmanya, and Sastha. No founding date or royal patron for the temple is documented in the sources consulted for this entry; its history before the modern devaswom-administration era survives mainly through legend and continuous local worship rather than inscriptions or chronicles.

Chottanikkara has also become widely known, including through popular film adaptations of its legend, for rites associated with healing those believed to be troubled by evil spirits or mental distress — though the specifics of those practices are not detailed here. Its ritual calendar centers on festivals such as Makam Thozhal, Navarathri, Thrikkarthika, and Deepavali.
