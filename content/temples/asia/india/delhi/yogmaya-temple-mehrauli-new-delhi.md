Tucked into Mehrauli just a few hundred yards from the Qutb Minar and its famous Iron Pillar, Yogmaya Temple is dedicated to the goddess Yogmaya -- worshipped here as an incarnation of Durga and, in Puranic tradition, as the sister of Krishna who is said to have eluded Kamsa at her own birth so that the infant Krishna could be safely spared. Temple tradition traces an original shrine on this site back to the Pandavas themselves, built in the years following the Mahabharata war, though that founding claim rests on devotional memory rather than independent historical or archaeological confirmation. What is better attested is the temple's medieval survival: it stands inside the walls of the old Lal Kot fortress, Delhi's earliest citadel, and is often described as the only one of the roughly two dozen temples destroyed under the early Delhi Sultanate's Mamluk rulers that has continued in active worship down to the present, having been restored under the Hindu ruler Hemu in the sixteenth century and further added to during Aurangzeb's reign.

The structure visitors see today, however, is considerably younger than any of those episodes: the temple was rebuilt in 1827, giving it its present form -- a modest walled enclosure with corner towers, at whose center a black stone idol sits in a marble-lined well beneath a stubby, copper-capped shikhara. Despite its small footprint, the temple carries an outsized cultural role in Delhi's calendar through the Phool Walon Ki Sair, an interfaith flower-sellers' festival held annually since 1812, in which floral fans are ceremonially offered both here and at the nearby dargah of the Sufi saint Qutb Sahib -- a pairing that has long made the festival a symbol of Delhi's shared Hindu-Muslim devotional culture. Around two hundred hereditary caretaker families continue to maintain the temple's daily rites.
