At Mangalagiri, in Guntur district, Vishnu is worshipped in Narasimha form as Thotadri — a name that also gives Mangalagiri itself its meaning, "the auspicious hill" (from Totadri). The temple complex, built in Dravidian style, links shrines at the base of the hill with further shrines set into the hillside above, and is counted among India's eight self-manifested (svayambhu) Narasimha kshetras. Temple tradition credits its discovery to Yudhishthira, eldest of the Pandava brothers, and traces its history to the Brahma Vaivarta Purana — a devotional account distinct from, and older than, the site's independently documented history.

That documented history begins clearly in the Vijayanagara period: stone inscriptions dated 1520 and 1538 CE record grants made under Krishnadevaraya and Sadasiva Raya respectively, when the area was known as Mangala Nilayam, and a separate inscription records a visit by Krishnadevaraya himself. Centuries later, Raja Vasireddy Venkatadri Nayudu of Amaravathi, a Narasimha devotee, built the temple's towering gopuram around 1807-1809 — roughly 153 feet and eleven storeys, among the tallest temple towers in South India.

A hillside shrine within the complex is the site of the temple's best-known ritual: jaggery water (panakam) is offered by conch to the deity there, and temple tradition holds the offering is partly and audibly consumed, a practice popularly attributed to neutralizing naturally occurring sulfur compounds on the hill. The temple's main annual festival is an eleven-day Brahmotsavam in February/March, whose closing day draws especially large crowds, alongside Sri Rama Navami, Narasimha Jayanti, and Vaikunta Ekadasi. Pilgrims reach the upper shrines via a stepped pathway built in 1890 or a ghat road opened in 2004; the site sits on National Highway 16 about 22 km northeast of Guntur, close to Vijayawada, with its own railway station on the Vijayawada-Guntur line.
