Umananda Temple occupies the whole of a small, steep island in the Brahmaputra -- Umananda, popularly called Peacock Island -- directly opposite Guwahati's riverfront and reachable only by boat. The temple's name fuses "Uma," Shiva's consort, with "ananda," bliss, and the site is identified in the Kalika Purana with Bhairavananda, a form Shiva is said to have taken while meditating here; the same body of legend holds that Shiva reduced the love-god Kamadeva to ashes on this hill after Kamadeva disturbed his meditation, giving the island its older name Bhasmachala, "hill of ashes." Evidence of an earlier stone temple from the post-Gupta period shows the site was already sacred before its best-documented history begins: the standing brick temple was built in 1694 under the Ahom king Gadadhar Singha, on the orders carried out by the noble Bar Phukan Garhganya Handique.

That temple did not survive intact. The great Assam earthquake of 1897 badly damaged the structure, and it was rebuilt afterward by a wealthy local merchant, whose reconstruction left an unusual trace: Vaishnavite inscriptions inside what remains, in dedication, a Shiva temple -- a small but telling sign of how freely Assam's devotional traditions have mixed on the ground. The temple had already weathered an earlier round of disruption under Mughal rule, when Gauhati's fall to Mughal forces brought desecration before a later jagir (land grant) from Aurangzeb restored some support to the shrine. Today the climb up from the ghat leads past rock-cut carvings that predate the brick temple to a small hilltop complex housing not only the main Shiva sanctum but ten further shrines to other deities, making Umananda a compact but densely layered pilgrimage stop just a short ferry ride from the city.
