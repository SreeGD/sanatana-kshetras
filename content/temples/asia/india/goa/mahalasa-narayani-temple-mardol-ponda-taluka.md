The Mahalasa Narayani Temple at Mardol, in Goa's Ponda taluka, enshrines one of the more unusual identities in the Vishnu tradition: Mahalasa is worshipped as Mohini, the female avatar Vishnu assumed during the churning of the cosmic ocean, and her four-armed image — bearing a trishula, sword, severed head, and drinking bowl — is dressed and venerated in both feminine and masculine forms of Vishnu across the ritual year, a fluidity of gender presentation that sets the deity apart from most Vaishnava shrines in this directory. The temple's present location is itself a product of Goa's colonial religious history: the original shrine stood at Old Mardol, also called Velham, in Verna, part of Salcette taluka, until it was destroyed by the Portuguese in 1567 during the systematic dismantling of Hindu temples in the territory then under their control. The sacred icon was rescued and, amid the continuing pressure of forced conversion in the Old Conquests, relocated during the 17th century to its present, more secure site in Mardol, in Ponda taluka — then outside direct Portuguese administration — a pattern of flight shared by numerous other Goan deities, including Shantadurga and Mangeshi, already documented elsewhere in this directory.

An older and less certain tradition traces the deity's origins even further back, holding that Mahalasa's worship travelled from Nepal through Aurangabad before ever reaching Goa; this earlier chapter is considerably less well attested than the documented 16th–17th century history of destruction and relocation within Goa itself, and is presented here strictly as oral tradition. The present temple complex reflects centuries of continuous devotion built up around the rescued icon: subsidiary shrines to Shantadurga and Lakshmi Narayan stand within the same compound alongside sanctuaries for five attendant deities, and the grounds hold a large ringerless brass bell once used for oath-taking and a prominent brass oil lamp. Sundays carry special ritual significance, marked by palakhi (palanquin) processions, while the Magha Jatra and Navaratri remain the temple's largest annual festivals.
