Thiruneermalai — literally "sacred hill of water" — is a Divya Desam built on a low rocky hillock that legend holds was once entirely surrounded by floodwaters, giving the site its name. Two separate shrines together form this one Divya Desam: Sri Neervanna Perumal Temple at the foot of the hill, where Vishnu stands facing the devotee, and a Ranganatha Perumal shrine at the summit, where he reclines. Between the two, and counting the standing and reclining forms, the site is celebrated for offering all four classical postures of Vishnu in one place — Nindra (standing, as Neervanna Perumal), Iruntha (seated, as Narasimha), Kidantha (reclining, as Ranganatha), and Nadantha (striding, as Trivikrama/Ulagalanda Perumal) — a combination devotees consider especially rare and auspicious.

According to temple legend, the sage Bhrigu and the long-lived rishi Markandeya, after having darshan of Vishnu reclining as Ranganatha at Srirangam, longed to behold that same form again; moved by their devotion, Vishnu is said to have appeared on this hill in the reclining "Bhoga Sayana" posture to grant their wish, establishing the hilltop Ranganatha shrine. The Vaishnava saint-poet Thirumangai Alvar is said to have journeyed here only to find the hill ringed by water, making the climb impossible; he waited, by tradition for as long as six months, until the floodwaters receded before he could finally have darshan of the Lord and compose his hymns — hymns in which he specifically marvels at witnessing Vishnu in multiple postures at a single site. Bhoothathalvar likewise sang in praise of the temple, securing its place in the Naalayira Divya Prabandham.

Historically, the temple carries numerous inscriptions from the Chola and later Pandya periods recording land grants and endowments, indicating sustained royal patronage over several centuries. Today Thiruneermalai functions as a quiet but living pilgrimage site on the western edge of the Chennai metropolitan area, its twin shrines and layered legend of flood, sage, and Alvar continuing to draw devotees from across the Sri Vaishnava tradition.
