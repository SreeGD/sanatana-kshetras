The Nagapooshani Amman Temple on the small island of Nainativu, just off the Jaffna peninsula, is one of the best-attested sites in this batch: a 12th-century Tamil inscription found at the site records an edict of the Sinhala king Parakramabahu I, giving firm evidence that the temple was already an active, recognized shrine by the mid-1100s, in a region long associated with serpent (naga) worship — reflected in the goddess's own name, Nagapooshani. The presiding goddess is also known as Bhuvaneswari, with the resident Shiva worshipped here under the local name Nayinaar. The original ancient structure did not survive into the modern era intact: Portuguese forces destroyed the temple in 1620 during their campaigns in the Jaffna kingdom, and the sanctuary standing today was rebuilt over roughly seven decades, between 1720 and 1790, meaning that while the site's sacred history stretches back centuries, none of its present architecture is itself ancient.

In wider Shakti Peetha literature, this temple is also known as the Indrakshi Temple and identified as the spot where Sati's anklets fell — one of the network of Shakti Peethas extending beyond the Indian subcontinent into Sri Lanka. It is worth noting, however, that this alternate name and legend appear only in a general reference list of the Shakti Peethas and are not discussed in the temple's own dedicated historical account, so the Shakti Peetha identity should be understood as a devotional tradition layered onto a temple whose primary, better-documented identity is as a major Amman shrine in its own right. Today the temple is a significant pilgrimage destination for both Hindu and Buddhist visitors from the region, with its 16-day Mahostavam festival in the Tamil month of Aani drawing over a hundred thousand pilgrims.
