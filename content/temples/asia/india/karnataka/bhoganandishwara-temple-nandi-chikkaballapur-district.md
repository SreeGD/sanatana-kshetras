At the base of Nandi Hills, a granite hill station northeast of Bengaluru long favored as a retreat by Tipu Sultan and, later, British administrators, stands one of Karnataka's oldest and least-visited-relative-to-its-importance temple complexes: the twin shrines of Bhoganandishwara and Arunachaleswara, both dedicated to Shiva. The northern and older of the two, the Bhoganandishwara temple, is reckoned the oldest surviving example of Nolamba-style Dravidian architecture in the state, built in the ninth or tenth century under the Nolamba dynasty, which governed this part of the Karnataka plateau before Chola and later Hoysala overlordship absorbed the region.

The complex grew across the centuries that followed, as successive dynasties — Ganga, Chola, Hoysala, and finally Vijayanagara rulers — added structures and endowments, so that the temple today reads as a layered record of South Indian temple architecture from the ninth through the sixteenth centuries: intricately carved pillars, a spacious sabha-mandapa, perforated stone jali screens, nandi-mantapas facing each sanctum, and pyramidal Dravida-style vimanas over the twin shrines. Between the two main temples sits a smaller Uma-Maheshwara shrine with its own kalyana-mandapa (marriage hall), which remains a particular draw for newly married couples seeking blessings — a living thread of worship continuing at a site the Archaeological Survey of India otherwise protects primarily as a monument.
