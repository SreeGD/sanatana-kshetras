Malinithan, on a modest hill overlooking the Brahmaputra's north bank at Likabali in Arunachal Pradesh's Lower Siang district, is one of the northeastern Himalayan foothills' more important archaeological witnesses to pre-modern Hindu and tribal religious life. Excavations here have uncovered a finely carved stone plinth, roughly eight feet high, bearing sculpted figures of Indra, Kartikeya, Surya, and Ganesha, alongside animal reliefs; stonemasons' marks link the site to the better-known Tamreswari temple and other ruins near Sadiya in Assam. Art-historical dating suggests religious activity on the hill from as early as the 10th century, with the main temple structure built under the Chutia kings during the 13th-14th centuries. The presiding deity is identified as Kechai-Khati (also called Pishasi), a fierce tribal goddess that scholars connect to Dikkaravasini of the Kalika Purana and to the broader current of Devi worship associated with northeastern Assam's Kamakhya tradition — suggesting the site began as an indigenous shrine that was later absorbed into, and elaborated by, Sanskritic temple architecture.

A separate and much later legend, tied to the 16th-century Ekasarana Vaishnava movement founded by the Assamese saint Srimanta Sankardev, recasts the site as a resting place for Krishna and his bride Rukmini during their flight from her intended, unwanted marriage — hosted there, the story goes, by Shiva and Parvati themselves — which is likely the source of the name "Malinithan." This narrative sits alongside, rather than replaces, the older indigenous Devi identification, and the two together illustrate how successive religious traditions layered new meaning onto the same sacred hill. Today Malinithan exists primarily as a protected archaeological site; its carved plinth and sculptures, along with evidence of what appear to be tantric-influenced carvings, remain its best-documented feature.
