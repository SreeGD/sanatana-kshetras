The Sarangapani Temple in Kumbakonam, known in the Divya Prabandham as Thirukkudanthai, is among the most architecturally prominent Divya Desams in the Chola Nadu group, with an 11-tier rajagopuram rising some 173 feet — reportedly the third tallest among all 108 Divya Desam towers — and a sanctum built in the form of a chariot, drawn by carved horses and elephants. The presiding deity, Sarangapani ("the one who holds the Sharanga bow"), also known in some sources as Aravamudhan, is worshipped with his consort Komalavalli Thayar, a form of Lakshmi.

Two linked legends explain the temple's origins. In one, the sage Bhrigu, testing the trimurti, struck Vishnu on the chest; offended, Lakshmi departed to be born on earth as Bhargavi (later identified with the temple's own goddess). In a complementary tradition, the sage Hemarishi undertook a long penance at this spot, and Lakshmi rose from the temple's Potramarai ("golden lotus") tank as the infant Komalavalli, whom Vishnu himself — descending in a celestial chariot as Aravamudhan — later married; the chariot-shaped sanctum is said to commemorate his descent. Kumbakonam's more famous legend of a nectar-pot giving the town its name belongs to a separate, nearby Shiva temple (Adi Kumbeswarar) associated with the Mahamaham festival, and should not be confused with this temple's own founding story.

Historically, the temple's inscriptions date from the late Chola period (10th-12th century), with major expansion under the Vijayanagara empire and the Thanjavur Nayaks between the 15th and 17th centuries; a modern Mahasamprokshanam re-consecration was held in 2015. The temple is glorified in the Nalayira Divya Prabandham by seven of the twelve Alvars, with Thirumangai Alvar contributing the largest share, 25 hymns. Sri Vaishnava tradition also credits this site with a pivotal role in the compilation of the entire Nalayira Divya Prabandham: hearing ten hymns of Nammalvar recited here is said to have prompted the 10th-century acharya Nathamuni to seek out and compile the full corpus of Alvar poetry.
