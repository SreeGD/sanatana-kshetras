Munneswaram, near Chilaw on Sri Lanka's northwestern coast, is a Shiva temple complex counted among the island's five traditional Pancha Ishwaram shrines. Numismatic evidence — coins issued in its name — shows it was already an established temple by the 11th century CE, apparently having grown from an earlier village shrine to a local guardian deity (Munisvaran) into a major Shaiva temple under royal patronage. Popular tradition also links the site to episodes of the Ramayana, including a visit by Rama, though this association is devotional rather than historically documented.

The temple was destroyed by Portuguese colonial forces in 1578 and again in the early 1600s — with local custodians reportedly hiding many of its images beforehand — and was subsequently rebuilt under the Kandyan king Rajasinghe I, with its superstructure fully restored and ceremonially reconsecrated by 1753 under King Kirti Sri Rajasinghe. Today the wider complex includes a separate, especially popular shrine to Kali alongside the main Shiva sanctum, and draws a notably multi-religious crowd: cited surveys found a large majority of pilgrims to be Sinhalese Buddhists, particularly at the Kali shrine, alongside the temple's core Hindu Tamil community. Its major annual observance is a month-long festival that likewise draws Hindus, Buddhists, Catholics, and Muslims, in addition to the more strictly Hindu observances of Navarathri and Maha Shivaratri.
