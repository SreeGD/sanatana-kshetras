Candi Sukuh, on the western slope of Mount Lawu in Central Java at roughly 910 meters elevation, is among the last major Hindu temples built in Java before Islam became the region's dominant religion, bearing a chronogram date on its western gate corresponding to 1437 CE, during the reign of the Majapahit empire's Queen Suhita (1429-1446). Unlike the towering, tiered candi of earlier Javanese Hindu architecture, Sukuh takes the unusual form of a stepped, truncated pyramid, a design so distinct from the regional norm that observers have sometimes compared it to Mesoamerican step pyramids, though the resemblance is coincidental. The site was first documented by Westerners in 1815 under Johnson during the British administration of Java, studied by the Dutch archaeologist Van der Vlis in 1842, and given its first restoration beginning in 1928; it has been registered as an Indonesian national cultural heritage site since March 26, 2007.

The temple's principal dedication is to Shiva, represented not by an anthropomorphic image but by a large stone lingga, originally about 1.82 meters tall and now preserved in Indonesia's National Museum in Jakarta, alongside prominent relief carvings of Ganesha. Sukuh is also notable among Javanese Hindu monuments for its explicit fertility- and sexuality-themed reliefs, widely interpreted as connected to purification rituals marking the temple's mountain setting and its position at the twilight of organized Hindu worship in Java. Today it functions primarily as an archaeological and heritage site administered by Indonesia's Ministry of Culture, though a square offering platform above the main temple still shows traces of burnt incense and offerings, indicating some visitors continue to use the site informally for prayer.
