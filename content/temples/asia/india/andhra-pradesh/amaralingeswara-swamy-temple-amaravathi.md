Amararama, the Amaralingeswara Swamy Temple at Amaravathi on the Krishna River in Palnadu district, is one of the five Pancharama Kshetras -- a set of Shiva temples sharing a common origin legend and, distinctively, the only one of the five not situated in the Godavari delta region. According to that shared tradition, the demon Tarakasura carried an invincible Atma-linga given to him by Shiva; when Kartikeya finally broke it apart with the agneyastra on Vishnu's counsel, the fragments are said to have been consecrated at five separate sites, each becoming a Pancharama shrine. Local tradition at Amaravathi ties the very name of the village to this event, holding that the devas (amaras) settled here afterward -- a devotional etymology rather than a documented one. The presiding deity, Amaralingeswara, is worshipped as a large white marble Shivalinga on Krouncha Saila hill, with Bala Chamundika Ammavaru as the temple's consort goddess.

Unlike some Pancharama sites, Amararama's documented history is unusually rich: thirty-five inscriptions survive on its walls, the earliest dated to 1129 CE, which is the primary epigraphical basis for placing the standing temple's construction around the 11th century -- a little later than the temple's Dravidian architecture, which echoes the roughly 10th-century Bhimeswara temples at Samalkota and Draksharamam, might otherwise suggest. Later inscriptions and additions trace a long history of continued patronage: a 1184 CE record of the Kota chief Keta II that also mentions a nearby Buddhist Mahachaitya and lamp gifts to the Buddha (evidence the site sat within a mixed Buddhist-Hindu religious landscape at the time), Reddi-dynasty work in the 14th century, a mandapam credited to the Vijayanagara emperor Krishnadevaraya in the early 16th century, and a substantial expansion funded by the local zamindar Vasireddy Venkatadri Nayudu in the late 18th century. Older still, Satavahana-period sculptures -- a Chaturmukha Linga, mukhalinga fragments, and a finely worked Nandi -- found near the temple suggest that Shaiva worship at this spot considerably predates the current 11th-century structure, a picture reinforced by roughly 1,800-year-old artifacts reportedly uncovered during a 2017 gopuram renovation. The nearby 21st-century planned capital of Andhra Pradesh, also named Amaravati, took its name from this much older Krishna-side village but is a distinct location about 35 km away.
