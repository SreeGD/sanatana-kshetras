Founded in the late 1800s — by internal arithmetic in the temple's own festival numbering, 1893 — by Dagdusheth Halwai, a sweet-shop owner originally from Gokak in Karnataka who had become a wealthy Pune businessman, this temple began as an act of grief and devotion. After he and his wife Laxmibai lost their only son to a plague epidemic, the couple built a Ganesh temple in his memory on the advice of a sage. Its public civic character owes much to Lokmanya Bal Gangadhar Tilak, who during the same period gave Ganesh Chaturthi celebrations their modern public form across Maharashtra as a way of organizing large gatherings under British rule without falling foul of bans on political assembly — a movement this temple's own Ganeshotsav became a flagship expression of.

Today the temple houses a gold-adorned idol some 2.2 metres tall, insured for roughly Rs 1 crore, and is run by the Shreemant Dagadusheth Halwai Sarvajanik Ganapati Trust, which also funds an old-age home and other charitable work in Pune. It draws over 100,000 devotees annually and, during Ganeshotsav, becomes one of the city's largest public festival sites, with its 132nd such celebration marked in 2024. Despite its fame as Pune's most iconic urban Ganesha shrine, it is explicitly separate from the region's Ashtavinayak circuit of eight historic Ganesha temples, none of which are located within Pune city itself.
