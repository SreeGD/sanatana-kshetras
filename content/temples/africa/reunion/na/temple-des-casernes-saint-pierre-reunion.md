The Temple des Casernes in Saint-Pierre, on Réunion's southern coast, occupies a repurposed building that originally belonged to the Usine des Casernes, a sugar factory, and dates in its present form to the first half of the twentieth century; French heritage records specify that the building was dedicated to Hindu worship from 1950. Like the island's other Tamil temples built around former sugar estates - a pattern rooted in the nineteenth-century "engagisme" indenture system that brought Tamil laborers to Réunion's plantations after the abolition of slavery in 1848 - this temple grew from an industrial site into a place of worship for the surrounding Tamil Hindu community.

Architecturally, the temple follows recognizably South Indian conventions in miniature: stone-cornered masonry beneath a corrugated-metal roof, kalasam finials, a square projecting apse decorated with bird reliefs on its gable, and an entrance portico, alongside a ritual "mât de Nargoulan" (a metal pole planted in concrete) and a fire-ritual area used in Tamil devotional practice. In recognition of this architectural and historical significance, the French state registered the Temple des Casernes in full as a monument historique on 17 September 2010. The temple's own presiding deity is not specified in the heritage records or secondary sources found for this entry, so its dedication is noted here only provisionally.
