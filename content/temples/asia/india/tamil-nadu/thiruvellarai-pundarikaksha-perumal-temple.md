Thiruvellarai's Pundarikaksha Perumal Temple is among the more archaeologically well-documented Divya Desams in the Chola Nadu group. Its sanctum stands atop a low rock outcrop, with two rock-cut cave shrines bearing inscriptions from the Pallava kings Nandivarman II and Dantivarman, dating the site's core construction to the late 8th century CE — a period consistently corroborated across independent sources, unlike some neighboring temples whose dating rests on more generic claims. Later inscriptions record continued patronage from the Cholas, Pandyas, Hoysalas, and the Vijayanagara empire. A distinctive monument on the temple grounds is a large swastika-shaped stepped well, popularly called the Marbidugu Kinaru, said to have been built around 800 CE under Dantivarman; it was damaged by floods in the 13th century and later restored during the Hoysala period, and is today a protected archaeological monument.

Two differing legends explain the deity's name, Pundarikaksha ("lotus-eyed"). In one telling, King Sibi Chakravarthi, chasing a wild boar, stumbled upon the sage Markandeya deep in penance; Vishnu appeared and demanded that 3,700 Vaishnavas be gathered to build a temple here, and when one devotee died en route and left the count short, Vishnu himself took on the form and name of a Vaishnava devotee called Pundarikakshan to complete it. A second, simpler tradition credits a devotee named Pundarikan, who tended a tulsi garden and worshipped Vishnu so devotedly that the Lord took his name. Both versions circulate in temple literature without being formally reconciled.

The temple is glorified in the Nalayira Divya Prabandham by Periyalvar and Thirumangai Alvar. Sri Vaishnava tradition also connects the site to later acharyas: the poet-theologian Vedanta Desika (13th-14th century) references Thiruvellarai in his work Hamsa Sandesam, and local tradition holds that Uyyakondar, a disciple of Nathamuni, was born here. The temple's annual chariot festival (Ratholsavam) in the Tamil month of Chittirai remains its principal living celebration.
