Sri Deepa Prakasar Perumal Temple, in the Thoopul quarter of Kanchipuram (the site also known by its older name Thiruthanka), enshrines Vishnu as Deepa Prakasar — "the one who is light itself" — also affectionately called Vilakkoli Perumal ("the lamp-light Lord") in Tamil. Sthala Purana tradition explains the name through a legend in which Vishnu appeared as a radiant light to dispel a moment of darkness or despair at this spot, an episode still recalled in local temple lore though it is not attested in an independent primary text. The deity is enshrined facing west in a standing (Nindra Thirukkolam) posture, with a separate shrine for his consort Maragathavalli Thayar within the temple's two precincts.

The temple's greatest claim to fame in Sri Vaishnava history is that it is the birthplace of Vedanta Desika (1268–1369 CE), one of the tradition's most celebrated poet-philosophers and polymaths, born here as Venkatanathan. A dedicated sannidhi for Vedanta Desika stands within the temple complex — built, according to temple tradition, by his son Nainacharya (Varadacharya) — depicting the acharya with the gyana mudra, alongside a shrine to Lakshmi Hayagriva reflecting Desika's particular devotion to that form of Vishnu. During the temple's annual Purattasi Brahmotsavam, the festival image associated with Vedanta Desika is taken in procession toward the great Varadaraja Perumal temple elsewhere in Kanchipuram, visibly linking the two shrines in the town's living ritual calendar.

The temple is counted among the 108 Divya Desams through a hymn of Thirumangai Alvar preserved in the Naalayira Divya Prabandham, and its architectural core — a Dravidian-style structure with a three-tiered rajagopuram — was expanded during the Chola and Vijayanagara periods; an inscription dated to the reign of Rajaraja Chola III (1223 CE) attests to royal attention to the shrine well before Vedanta Desika's birth here later in that same century. Ranked among the larger temple complexes of southern Kanchipuram, it remains, for Sri Vaishnavas, sacred less for grandeur than for its unique standing as the earthly birthplace of one of the tradition's foremost teachers.
