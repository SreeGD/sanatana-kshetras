Prasat Sikhoraphum, in Surin Province in northeastern Thailand, is a well-preserved 12th-century Khmer Hindu temple built under King Suryavarman II during the height of the Angkorian Empire's expansion beyond present-day Cambodia. Its name derives from the Sanskrit "shikhara," referring to the temple's tower-like spires, and its brick sanctuary towers are richly decorated with carved apsaras (celestial dancers), devatas, and dvarapala guardian figures, iconography characteristic of Angkorian-era Shaiva Hindu temple architecture.

Like many Khmer Hindu temples on what is now Thai soil, Prasat Sikhoraphum was converted for Buddhist use in the 16th century as the region's religious landscape shifted, and it functions today primarily as a heritage and archaeological site rather than an active place of Hindu worship. It remains, however, one of the better-preserved examples of Angkorian Hindu temple carving in Thailand's Isan region, situated conveniently beside Sikhoraphum's own railway station between the towns of Surin and Sisaket.
