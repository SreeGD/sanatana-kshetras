Ramtek takes its name from a tradition that this hill was Rama's own dwelling ("Rama" + "tek," hill) during part of his forest exile - the story goes that after killing the ascetic Shambuka on the hill, whose unauthorised penance had caused the death of a Brahmin's son, Rama transformed him into a Shiva linga still worshipped today as Dhumreshwar Mahadev, and made the hill his abode. This devotional account, rather than the Vakataka-era archaeology, is what gives the site its Ramayana identity and its place on the modern Ramayana Circuit.

The hill's documented religious history reaches back much further than that legend, however. From the 5th century, under the Vakataka dynasty, Ramtek grew into a major centre of Vaishnava worship, particularly of Vishnu's Narasimha avatar, evidenced by structures such as the Kevala Narasimha Temple and the twin-sanctum Bhogarama Temple. Substantial rebuilding of the Rama and Lakshmana temples that dominate the hilltop today took place in the Yadava period, in the late 13th century, and the site's fortunes were transformed again in the 18th century when Raghuji I, the first Maratha raja of Nagpur, fortified the summit and renovated the Rama-Lakshmana shrines, work that gave Ramtek the "all-India" pilgrimage standing it has held since. The temples display the gently curving shikharas typical of the Hemadpanthi style, and the fortified hilltop is entered through a sequence of four gates - Varaha, Singhpur, Bhairava, and Gokula Darwaza - with a Jain Shantinath temple also standing within the complex, a reminder of Ramtek's long history as a shared sacred site.

Ramtek carries one further, more scholarly claim to fame: the 20th-century Sanskritist V. V. Mirashi argued that this hill is the "Ramagiri" from which the exiled Yaksha addresses the cloud-messenger in Kalidasa's Meghaduta - a proposition Wikipedia describes as unproven but the strongest among several candidates. That association is commemorated today in the Kalidas Smarak (Kalidasa Memorial) near the hill and an annual festival in the poet's honour. The temple complex remains an active and heavily visited pilgrimage site, with Rama Navami and Kartik Purnima - when the waters of the adjoining Ambala Lake are held locally to be as sacred as the Ganga - drawing tens of thousands of pilgrims each year.
