Thiruvadariyaachiramam, the holy hermitage of Badari, is revered as one of the 108 Divya Desams and the most celebrated of the northern abodes of Vishnu, set high amid the Himalayas. The presiding deity, Badri Narayanan, is worshipped in a seated, meditative posture, and the kshetra is venerated as the place where the Lord undertook penance for the welfare of the world. According to the sthala purana, this is the eternal ashram of Nara and Narayana, the twin sages who are understood as forms of the Lord engaged in austerity amid the badari (jujube) groves that gave the place its name. Surrounded by snow-clad peaks and sanctified by the river that flows beside it, Badari is held to be a region where divine and ascetic life meet, drawing pilgrims across the ages on an arduous northern journey. Among the Alwars, Periyalwar and Thirumangai Alwar sang the glory of this distant shrine in the Naalayira Divya Prabandham, bringing the far Himalayan abode into the devotional landscape of the Tamil saints alongside the temples of the south. Thirumangai Alwar in particular, whose verses range widely over the sacred geography of the north, extolled Thiruvadariyaachiramam as a place where the Lord may be sought in His austere and gracious form. As a Divya Desam, Badari stands as a powerful reminder that the abodes sung by the Alwars are not confined to the Tamil country but extend to the very heights of the Himalayas, where the Lord abides for the redemption of all beings.

The temple's documented institutional history begins with Adi Shankara, who established Badrinath as an organized pilgrimage site in the 9th century CE. The murti itself was moved to its present location in the 16th century by a King of Garhwal, and the temple complex was further expanded under later Garhwal rulers in the 17th century. A major turning point came after the 1803 Garhwal earthquake damaged the structure: it was substantially rebuilt with funding from the King of Jaipur, with renovation work continuing through the 1870s and effectively completed before the First World War. The temple as it stands today comprises three linked sections — the garbhagriha (sanctum), Darshan Mandapa, and Sabha Mandapa — with the sanctum's conical roof rising about 15 meters to a small gilt-covered cupola, reached via a broad stairway and tall arched gateway. Inside, a roughly one-foot black-granite Shaligram image of Badrinarayan, seated in a lotus posture holding a conch and discus, rests beneath a gold canopy traditionally said to stand beneath a badari (jujube) tree; the sanctum also houses images of Kubera, Narada, Uddhava, Nara-Narayana, and some fifteen further deities including Lakshmi, Garuda, and the Navadurga. Adjoining the temple, the Tapt Kund hot springs maintain a year-round temperature of about 55°C and are traditionally used by pilgrims for a purifying bath before darshan.

## Festivals

As a high Himalayan shrine of the Lord reclining as Badri Narayanan, the temple's life follows the rhythm of the mountains. Worship is possible only during the open season, and its great moments are the ceremonial opening of the gates on Akshaya Tritiya in spring, when the deity is bathed and adorned, and the solemn closing around Bhai Dooj in autumn, when an Akhand Jyoti — a ghee lamp meant to burn continuously through the temple's six-month winter closure — is lit before the doors are shut. Within the season, devotees traditionally observe the Vaishnava cycle of festivals, including the annual Brahmotsavam, Vaikunta Ekadasi, and special abhishekams and recitation of sacred hymns, alongside the Mata Murti ka Mela (commemorating the Ganga's descent to earth) and the eight-day, June-held Badri-Kedar Festival shared with Kedarnath, which draws performing artists from across India. The Lord, glorified by the Azhwars as one of the few divine seats of the Vada Nadu, draws pilgrims for the Char Dham yatra, with the temple recording as many as 2.8 million visits in just two months during 2022.

## Location & Access

The temple stands at Badrinath, in Chamoli district, Uttarakhand, at an elevation of roughly 3,100 meters in the upper Alaknanda valley, and is one of the five shrines of the Panch Badri group. Rishikesh has the nearest railhead and Dehradun's Jolly Grant airport the nearest air access, with the final stretch covered by mountain road via Joshimath. Being a high-altitude shrine, it opens only seasonally (Akshaya Tritiya through the Bhai Dooj period, roughly late April to early November) and, since 2012, uses a token system to manage the large crowds passing before the deity. Verify current darshan timings and travel details with the temple or official sources before visiting.
