Built by Trinidadian devotee Sewdass Sadhu (b. 1901), an indentured laborer, this temple — also known as the Sewdass Sadhu Shiva Mandir — stands on an artificial islet just offshore, reached by a causeway. In the 1930s Sadhu had first built a small shoreline shrine ("sewalla") dedicated to Shiva on land owned by the Tate and Lyle Sugar Company; after it was demolished by the estate's management (he was imprisoned 14 days and fined 100 pounds for refusing to remove it), he resolved instead to build on land no one could take from him, the sea itself, transporting stones, cement, and sand out to the site by bicycle starting in 1947 and completing the rocky causeway and offshore temple in 1952. Following Sadhu's death in 1970, the temple was rebuilt under Randolph Rampersad's leadership and reopened in 1995, marking the 150th anniversary of Indian indentured laborers' first arrival in Trinidad. It houses idols of Shiva, Hanuman, Ganesha, Durga, and Krishna, and stands as an emblem of the resilience of Trinidad's Indo-Caribbean Hindu community, descended from 19th-century indentured laborers.
