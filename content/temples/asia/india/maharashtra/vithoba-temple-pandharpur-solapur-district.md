Pandharpur is the geographic and devotional center of the Warkari tradition, a centuries-old Maharashtrian bhakti movement built around pilgrimage on foot rather than doctrine alone. Twice a year, ahead of Ashadhi and Kartiki Ekadashi, tens of thousands of pilgrims walk in organized processions called dindis — many covering well over 200 km from towns across the state — converging on the town to see Vithoba, a form of Vishnu, standing in the sanctum beside his consort Rakhumai (Rukmini). The temple's founding story is themselves inseparable from this tradition: Pundalik, a son so devoted to caring for his elderly parents that he would not pause his service even for a divine visitor, is said to have tossed Krishna a brick to stand on while he finished his duties — and Krishna, moved by that devotion, agreed to remain standing on the brick at Pandharpur ever after, worshipped in that fixed, waiting posture as Vithoba.

The temple's documented history stretches back further than most Deccan shrines of comparable stature: a shrine to Vithoba is attested from 1189 CE, with expansion recorded around 1273-77, and a surviving 1237 CE inscription records a Hoysala king's grant of a village to fund the temple's upkeep — evidence that Vithoba worship at Pandharpur already had royal patronage by the 13th century. The structure seen today, however, is a composite: later Deccan-style features such as domed motifs and lobed arches were added from the 17th century onward, so it is best understood as a building that has grown in layers over roughly eight centuries rather than one raised at a single moment. In 2014 the temple trust took the notable step of permitting women and priests from historically marginalized castes to perform rituals, a departure from earlier practice that drew wide attention within the Warkari community.
