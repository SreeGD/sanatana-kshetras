Sri Ranganathaswamy Temple at Srirangam is revered as the foremost of the 108 Divya Desams — first in the traditional sequence and, by area, one of the largest functioning Hindu temple complexes in the world, spanning roughly 63 hectares with seven concentric prakarams (enclosures), 21 gopurams, and 81 shrines. The presiding deity, Ranganatha, is Vishnu reclining on the serpent Adishesha, with consort Ranganayaki (Lakshmi) enshrined separately. The town's traditional name, Thiruvarangam, gives the temple its other common name, and it is popularly called "Bhooloka Vaikuntam" — Vishnu's abode on earth.

According to sthala purana tradition, the temple's vimana (sanctum tower) originated with Brahma, who worshipped it during the churning of the ocean, and it later passed through the Ikshvaku dynasty to Rama, who gifted it to Vibhishana, king of Lanka, as a token of gratitude after the war. Carrying the vimana homeward, Vibhishana rested it on the banks of the Kaveri at Srirangam; when he tried to lift it again, the deity is said to have declined to move further, choosing to remain here facing south — toward Lanka — as a boon to Vibhishana. This legend explains the temple's unusual south-facing sanctum and its standing as one of the very few Anantasayana (reclining Vishnu) shrines oriented this way.

Historically, the site's antiquity is well attested: excavated mandapas in the vicinity have been dated to roughly 100-300 CE, corresponding to the early Chola era centered at nearby Uraiyur, and more than 600 surviving inscriptions — out of some 800 recorded historically — document continuous royal patronage from the Cholas, Pandyas, and Hoysalas through the Vijayanagara empire between the 7th and 17th centuries. The temple was a major center of Sri Vaishnava theology: the acharyas Nathamuni, Yamunacharya, and especially Ramanuja (who lived and taught here for decades in the 11th-12th centuries) shaped its ritual and philosophical life. A documented historical trauma is the 1311 CE raid by the Delhi Sultanate general Malik Kafur, during which the principal festival image was reportedly removed north for safekeeping and only later recovered and reinstalled; further incursions followed in the 1320s before Vijayanagara-era rulers restored and expanded the temple from the late 14th century onward. In 2017 the temple received a UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award of Merit for conservation, and it remains on India's UNESCO World Heritage tentative list.

The temple is celebrated throughout the Nalayira Divya Prabandham, with tradition holding that it is the only Divya Desam glorified in hymn by all twelve Alvar saints. Its most spectacular living festival is the 21-day Vaikunta Ekadashi (Adhyayana Utsavam) each Margazhi (December-January), when the sacred verses of the Prabandham are ceremonially recited before the deity and pilgrims pass through the temple's Paramapada Vasal ("gateway to the highest heaven") — a ritual understood as a symbolic passage to Vaikuntha itself.
