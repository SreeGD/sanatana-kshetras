In a village of Himachal Pradesh's remote Lahaul and Spiti district, about 6 km south of the Chenab river and 9 km from Udaipur, the Trilokinath Temple stands as one of the Himalayas' most distinctive shared shrines: a single sanctum venerated simultaneously by Tibetan Buddhists, who regard its central marble image as Avalokiteshvara, the Bodhisattva of compassion, and by Hindus, who worship the same image as Trilokinath, "Lord of the Three Worlds," a form of Shiva. The temple's material fabric reflects this duality directly — large Buddhist prayer wheels stand alongside a granite Shiva lingam and a small Nandi bull image within the same precinct.

Accounts of the temple's origin diverge: local tradition holds it began life as a Buddhist vihara, while some scholars propose the opposite, that it was originally a Shiva temple converted to Buddhist worship by the tantric master Padmasambhava in the 8th century. Its curvilinear stone shikhara, crowned with an amalaka finial, points to a 7th-8th century regional building tradition, and the central marble image itself is attributed by some to the 12th century. Whatever its precise origin, the temple's present-day identity as a living site of joint worship is well established: each August, both Buddhist and Hindu communities of Lahaul come together for the three-day Pauri Festival, a rare and enduring instance of shared devotion in the high Himalayas.
