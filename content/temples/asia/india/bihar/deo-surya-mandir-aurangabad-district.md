Deo Surya Mandir, in Deo town in Bihar's Aurangabad district, is one of India's most significant living Sun temples and, unusually among Surya shrines, faces west toward the setting sun rather than east toward the sunrise -- a feature that lets pilgrims offer both dawn and dusk arghya (water offerings) from the same spot across the temple's Chhath Puja season. A Gupta-period inscription dated 642 CE already attests to sun worship at this site, and a later inscription from 1437 CE records a dedication by a ruler named Bhairavendra, giving the temple a documented devotional history stretching back well over a millennium even though the Archaeological Survey of India's own dating of the present standing structure is inconsistently reported -- variously placed in the 5th-6th century or the 8th century in the sources reviewed for this entry. Local tradition credits the temple's construction to Vishwakarma, the divine architect of Hindu mythology, while some scholars have separately proposed that the structure may have begun as a Buddhist temple before being converted to Surya worship, a hypothesis presented here as unresolved rather than established fact.

Today Deo Surya Mandir is best known as a major regional focus of Chhath Puja, the distinctively Bihari-Purvanchali festival of Sun worship: during the six days following Diwali, lakhs of devotees from across Bihar converge on the temple's Surya Kund tank to perform the ritual dawn and dusk offerings central to the festival, with a smaller repeat observance at Chaiti Chhath in the month of Chaitra. Architecturally the temple blends Nagara, Dravidian, and Vesara elements beneath a domed tower crowned with a gold urn -- an eclectic style that, together with its west-facing orientation, marks it out from better-known Sun temples such as Konark (already documented elsewhere in this directory) even as both sites attest to a long, geographically dispersed tradition of solar worship in the Hindu tradition.
