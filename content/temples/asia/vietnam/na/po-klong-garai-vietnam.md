Po Klong Garai Temple, on a hill overlooking Phan Rang-Thap Cham in Ninh Thuan Province, is among the most significant surviving Cham Hindu monuments in Vietnam -- significant not only for its architecture but because it remains, unusually for a site of its age, a living place of Cham religious practice rather than a purely archaeological ruin. Built toward the end of the 13th century by King Jaya Simhavarman III, with steles suggesting an earlier structure at the site, the temple follows Shaivism and centers on a mukhalinga image that Cham tradition identifies with the deified King Po Klong Garai, who ruled Champa in the 12th-13th century and is credited in legend with major irrigation works that benefited the Cham kingdom.

The complex comprises three brick towers -- a three-story main sanctuary, a gate tower, and an elongated tower with a distinctive saddle-shaped roof -- built in the Thap Mam style of later Cham art. So central is the temple to the region's identity that the city of Phan Rang-Thap Cham takes half its name directly from it ("Thap Cham" meaning "Cham Tower"). Today designated a Special National Site of Vietnam, Po Klong Garai continues to host Cham religious festivals, including the annual Kate festival, making it one of the country's rare points of continuity between the Champa kingdom's Hindu past and living Cham Balamon religious practice in the present.
