Thiruppullani takes both its Tamil and Sanskrit names, Pullani and Dharbasayanam, from the same episode of the Ramayana: on his march south toward Lanka, Rama is said to have lain in penance and prayer on a bed of sacred darbha grass (pul) at this spot on the shore near Rameswaram, seeking the ocean god's leave to build the causeway, Rama Setu, that would carry his army across. The presiding deity, standing in this place of that legendary vigil, is worshipped as Adi Jagannathar, also known as Kalyana Jagannathar, with the consort venerated as Padmasini Thayar; devotees troubled by delays or obstacles in marriage are still said to seek Kalyana Jagannathar's blessing here.

The temple is glorified in the Naalayira Divya Prabandham through roughly twenty verses of Thirumangai Alvar's Periya Thirumozhi, placing Thiruppullani among the Pandya Nadu Divya Desams sung in the Tamil Vaishnava canon of the 6th to 9th centuries CE. Built in its present form from around the late 8th century, the temple carries inscriptional evidence of continuous royal patronage across several dynasties: a 1305 CE record of the Pandya king Maravarman Sundara Pandyan naming a minister, Arya Chakravarthi, and further inscriptions from the Madurai Nayak period in the early 16th century, in addition to later support from the Ramnad Sethupathi rulers whose seat at nearby Ramanathapuram made them long-standing patrons of temples along this stretch of coast. A metal image of Krishna dated to the 13th-century Pandya period is preserved in the temple, and its Chakra Theertham tank and five-tiered eastern gateway remain notable features of the complex.

Set on the pilgrim road between Ramanathapuram and Rameswaram, Thiruppullani today functions as both a Divya Desam in its own right and, through its Rama-Setu legend, an entry point to the Ramayana-linked sacred geography that culminates at Rameswaram itself, with its Panguni Brahmotsavam and Rama Navami festivals drawing pilgrims travelling that same route.
