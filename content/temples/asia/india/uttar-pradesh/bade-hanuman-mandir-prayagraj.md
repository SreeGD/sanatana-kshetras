Temple tradition traces the origin of the image to a childless merchant from Kannauj who commissioned a Hanuman idol carved from Vindhyachal stone; after he left it at Prayagraj during a pilgrimage, he was blessed with a son, and the idol was later recovered and installed at its present site by the ascetic Baba Balagiri Ji. A separate strand of tradition, dated to 1582, holds that the Mughal emperor Akbar tried to move the temple to make way for fort construction, but the image would not budge, so he built his wall around it instead and set aside the surrounding land for the shrine.

What makes the temple physically distinctive is the roughly 20-foot image of Hanuman shown reclining rather than standing or seated, said to be unlike any other Hanuman murti in India. Because the shrine sits close to the Ganga near the Sangam, the idol is partially submerged by rising floodwaters during the monsoon each year, a phenomenon devotees read as an expression of Hanuman's humility. The temple's popularly cited age of roughly 700 years is a traditional figure rather than an independently documented one, and it continues to draw large numbers of pilgrims heading to the Sangam, with renovation and expansion work underway ahead of the 2025 Kumbh Mela.
