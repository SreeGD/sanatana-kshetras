Kalkaji Mandir, opposite the Nehru Place business district in south Delhi, is one of the capital's most visited Shakta shrines, dedicated to the goddess Kali. Devotional tradition holds that this is where Kali, after being invoked to fight the demon Raktabija, defeated him by drinking his blood before it could touch the earth and spawn a fresh demon from every fallen drop — a version of the Raktabija episode more widely told in the context of Durga's battle with Mahishasura's forces. While the site is popularly regarded as very old, the surviving architectural fabric is documented only from around 1764 CE; the sanctum was enclosed in red sandstone and marble railings around 1783 through the patronage of one Durga Singh, and in 1816 a dome and twelve additional outer rooms were funded by Mirza Raja Kidarnath, a Peshkar (official) under the Mughal emperor Akbar II.

The temple's continued significance in a rapidly modernized part of Delhi is underscored by the discovery, in 1966, of a Minor Rock Edict of the emperor Ashoka at nearby Bahapur, dated to the 3rd century BCE — evidence that the area has been continuously significant for over two millennia, even if it does not by itself confirm an ancient temple on the present site. Today Kalkaji Mandir draws especially large crowds during both Chaitra and Sharad Navratri, remaining one of Delhi's principal centers of Shakti worship alongside Jhandewalan and Chhatarpur.
