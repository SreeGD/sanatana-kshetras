Belur Math sits on the west bank of the Hooghly river in Howrah district, directly facing Kolkata, and serves as the headquarters of the Ramakrishna Math and Ramakrishna Mission, the monastic and philanthropic order founded in 1897 by Swami Vivekananda. Vivekananda, chief disciple of the nineteenth-century Bengali mystic Sri Ramakrishna Paramahamsa, established the monastery here in the years following his celebrated 1893 appearance at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago, and the campus today functions simultaneously as a working monastery, a major pilgrimage and spiritual-tourism destination, and the administrative center for the Ramakrishna Mission's much larger global network of hospitals, schools, and relief work. It stands more or less directly across the river from Dakshineswar Kali Temple, where Ramakrishna himself served as a priest — a geographic pairing that mirrors the spiritual lineage running from Dakshineswar to Belur.

The campus's centerpiece, the Sri Ramakrishna Temple, was designed by Swami Vijnanananda — a direct monastic disciple of Ramakrishna who trained as a civil engineer — carrying out a plan Vivekananda himself had envisioned; its foundation stone was laid in March 1929 and the temple was consecrated in January 1938, some years after both Ramakrishna and Vivekananda had died. Rather than following a single regional temple style, the building deliberately fuses forms associated with several religions: a gateway recalling the Buddhist stupa at Sanchi, a silhouette drawing on the south Indian gopuram tradition, domes echoing Mughal and Rajput architecture, and a cross-shaped ground plan read as a nod to Christian church design. This syncretism is not incidental decoration but a built statement of Ramakrishna's own teaching that the world's religions are different paths to the same truth — the temple enshrines a marble image of Ramakrishna on a lotus pedestal said to contain his relics, and separate shrines elsewhere on the campus similarly honor his wife and spiritual consort Sarada Devi and Vivekananda himself.

The math remains intensely active today: it draws pilgrims and visitors year-round, and its Durga Puja, which includes a Kumari Puja (worship of a young girl as a living embodiment of the Goddess) that Vivekananda instituted in 1901, is among its most distinctive living observances.
