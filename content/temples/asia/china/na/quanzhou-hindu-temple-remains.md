During the late Song and Yuan dynasties, Quanzhou (then known to Arab and Persian traders as Zayton) was one of the world's great cosmopolitan seaports, home to resident merchant communities from across maritime Asia and the Islamic world. Among them was a community of Tamil Hindu merchants, evidently prosperous enough to build at least one substantial Hindu temple in the South Indian architectural idiom of the period. Surviving evidence points to construction in the early 1280s: a bilingual Tamil-Chinese inscription dated to April 1281 CE is associated with a dedication to Shiva, while a separate record describes a Vishnu temple built in 1283 CE by a Tamil merchant guild — it is not entirely clear from the sources reviewed here whether these describe one temple, two temples, or a single complex housing both deities.

The original Hindu structure(s) no longer stand, but more than 300 carved stone fragments — pillars, panels, and images carved from local granite in a style closely paralleling contemporaneous Chola-era temples in Tamil Nadu — survive scattered across roughly five sites in and around the city. A number of these carvings, including sculpted columns, were later built into the existing Buddhist Kaiyuan Temple (itself founded centuries earlier, in the Tang dynasty) behind its main hall, where they remain visible today as one of the few places in China where Hindu religious carvings are incorporated into an active Buddhist temple. Others are held in the Quanzhou Museum. No Hindu community has remained in Quanzhou since this diaspora-era trading community dispersed, so the site is treated here purely as an archaeological and heritage legacy rather than a living place of Hindu worship.
