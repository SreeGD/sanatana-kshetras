Nanpaya Temple stands in Myinkaba village, just south of the old walled city of Bagan, and is among the oldest surviving Hindu (Brahmanical) structures in the wider Bagan archaeological zone — a rare monument to classical Hinduism amid a landscape otherwise dominated by Buddhist stupas and temples. Its sandstone interior is carved with intricate Brahmanical iconography drawing on both the Vishnu and Brahma traditions of Hindu worship, most strikingly in its four central pillars, each carved with a three-headed image of Brahma seated on a lotus.

Local tradition attributes the temple's construction to King Manuha (Makuta), the captive Mon monarch of the defeated Thaton Kingdom, said to have built it as his personal residence during captivity in Bagan before it was converted into a temple. Distinct from the Vishnu-dedicated Nathlaung Kyaung Temple inside Bagan's old city walls (also documented in this directory), Nanpaya represents a second strand of the same brief flowering of Hindu religious architecture at Bagan, this one centered on Brahma iconography rather than Vishnu. Today it survives purely as a heritage and archaeological monument within the UNESCO World Heritage-listed Bagan zone, not as an active site of worship.
