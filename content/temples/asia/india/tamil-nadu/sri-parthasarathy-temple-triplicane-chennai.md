Sri Parthasarathy Temple, in the Thiruvallikeni (Triplicane) locality of central Chennai, is one of the most celebrated of the 108 Divya Desams and holds a unique distinction among all Vishnu temples: its principal deity is worshipped in the form of Parthasarathy, Krishna as the charioteer of Arjuna during the Mahabharata war. Tradition attributes the temple's founding to the Pallava dynasty — accounts vary between the reign of Narasimhavarman I in the 7th century and later Pallava rulers — with the earliest surviving epigraphical anchor being a grant inscription of the Pallava king Dantivarman (reigned c. 796–847 CE) dated 808 CE. The temple was subsequently enlarged over centuries: the Cholas, including Raja Raja Chola and Kulottunga III, made significant contributions, and the Vijayanagara kings Sadasiva Raya, Sriranga Raya, and Venkatapati Raya II added mandapas and subshrines in the 15th and 16th centuries, giving the temple much of its present layout.

The temple is remarkable for uniting five forms of Vishnu within a single complex: Yoga Narasimha, Rama, Gajendra Varada (Varadaraja), Ranganatha, and Krishna as Parthasarathy, allowing a single visit to honor deities otherwise associated with separate, far-flung Divya Desams such as Ahobilam, Ayodhya, Srirangam, and Kanchipuram. The moolavar (fixed) image is worshipped as Venkatakrishnan, while the processional utsavar form is Parthasarathy himself — depicted, unusually for any Krishna image, with a moustache and bearing a conch, recalling his martial role as charioteer rather than his more familiar pastoral or infant forms. Temple tradition holds that a mark on the deity's cheek commemorates the arrow wound Krishna sustained from Bhishma while shielding Arjuna, a detail devotees connect directly to the Mahabharata narrative.

Parthasarathy Temple is glorified in the Naalayira Divya Prabandham through hymns of Thirumangai Alvar and Peyalvar, and the temple maintains a shrine to Andal, the sole female Alvar, reflecting its deep integration into the wider Sri Vaishnava devotional and literary tradition. In the Vaishnava guruparampara, the temple is also associated with the 15th–16th century Acharya Manavala Mamunigal, commemorated in the annual Manavala Mamunigal Utsavam. Continuously worshipped for over a millennium in what is now a dense urban neighborhood of Chennai, the temple remains one of the city's most visited religious landmarks and a key pilgrimage stop within the Nadu Nadu group of Divya Desams.
