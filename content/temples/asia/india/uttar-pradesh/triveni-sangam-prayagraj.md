The Triveni Sangam ("triple confluence") at Prayagraj, the city known for most of its history as Allahabad, is among the most revered sites in all of Hinduism: the visible meeting point of the Ganga and Yamuna rivers, joined according to tradition by a third, mythical river, the Saraswati, believed to flow in invisibly from underground. The two visible rivers are traditionally distinguished by color — the Ganga running paler, the Yamuna darker — and pilgrims bathing at their meeting point are held, per verses invoked in the site's devotional tradition, to earn merit unavailable at either river alone. This confluence is the principal venue of the Prayag Kumbh Mela, held here once every twelve years and considered one of the largest peaceful gatherings of people anywhere in the world, alongside the annual Magh Mela and the intermediate Ardh Kumbh — placing Prayagraj among Hinduism's four rotating Kumbh sites, together with Haridwar, Ujjain, and Nashik-Trimbakeshwar (all separately documented elsewhere in this directory). The Sangam's standing extends into modern civic memory as well: it has been the site chosen for immersing the ashes of national figures, including Mahatma Gandhi in 1948 and former prime minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee in 2018.

A short distance away, inside Allahabad Fort, stands the Akshayavat ("imperishable banyan"), a sacred fig tree the Padma Purana describes as indestructible and which the Jain tradition holds as the spot where Rishabhanatha, the first Tirthankara, attained omniscience — a Svetambara shrine there preserves his footprints. The tree's survival is itself part of its legend: the Mughal emperor Jahangir is said to have had it cut down and its stump capped with a red-hot iron cauldron to prevent regrowth, only for it to sprout again within the year. Its history since is not entirely straightforward — a 19th-century British traveller recorded that local worshippers of the time doubted the tree being shown to the public was genuine, believing the original had been sealed off after the site passed into British control — and visiting it today still requires special permission from the fort's commandant, except for a brief window during the Kumbh Mela.
