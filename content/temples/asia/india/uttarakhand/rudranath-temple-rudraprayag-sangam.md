Rudraprayag, the fourth of the Panch Prayag counting downstream from Vishnuprayag, marks the confluence of the Alaknanda and the Mandakini, the river that itself descends from Kedarnath. The town's name invokes Rudra, the fierce aspect of Shiva, and local tradition holds that Shiva performed his cosmic Tandava dance here and played his favored instrument, the Rudra veena — the confluence is also said to be where the sage Narada meditated in order to learn music from Shiva in this form. Two shrines stand at the confluence itself: a Rudranath Temple dedicated to Shiva, and a Chamunda Devi Mandir dedicated to Chamunda, understood in this setting as Rudra's consort.

This Rudranath Temple should not be confused with the far better-known Rudranath of the Panch Kedar, a separate high-altitude shrine reached only by trek elsewhere in Chamoli district (documented in this directory under the Panch Kedar Tungnath entry's family of sister shrines) — the name is shared, but the two sites, and their local traditions, are distinct. No construction date or founding history survives for either shrine at the Rudraprayag confluence in the sources checked here.

Rudraprayag town is also historically notable well outside its religious associations as the hunting ground of the man-eating "Leopard of Rudraprayag," chronicled by the hunter-naturalist Jim Corbett. Today the town is a district headquarters and a standard waypoint on the Char Dham Yatra: it sits directly on National Highway 58, about 141 km from Rishikesh via Devprayag and Srinagar (Garhwal), with pilgrims commonly stopping to bathe at the confluence and visit the riverside shrines before continuing toward Kedarnath or Badrinath.
