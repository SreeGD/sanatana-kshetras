Mahanandi, on the eastern slopes of the Nallamala hills in present-day Nandyal district, is built around a natural phenomenon rather than an installed image alone: a self-manifested Shiva linga in the sanctum sits directly above a perennial spring, so that clear water continuously wells up around the base of the linga itself and feeds the temple's principal tank, maintaining a constant depth of roughly five feet through every season, including the dry months when other water sources in the surrounding Rayalaseema region run low. Tradition and available historical accounts trace an original structure at the site to the Badami Chalukya dynasty around the seventh century CE, with further building and endowment activity recorded in the tenth and fifteenth centuries as the shrine's reputation grew.

Mahanandi is also the senior member of a cluster of nine related Shiva shrines scattered within about fifteen kilometers of one another, collectively known as the Nava Nandi temples — among them Sivanandi, Vinayakanandi, Somanandi, Prathamanandi, Garudanandi, Suryanandi, Krishnanandi, and Naganandi — each associated by tradition with a different sage or deity said to have worshipped Shiva at that spot. The group as a whole, anchored by Mahanandi's ever-flowing spring, remains an active pilgrimage circuit, with Maha Shivaratri and Kartika Purnima the year's principal gathering days.
