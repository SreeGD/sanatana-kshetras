Perched at the edge of a roughly 70-meter cliff on the southwestern tip of Bali's Bukit Peninsula, Pura Uluwatu is one of Bali's directional temples (Pura Kahyangan Jagat), traditionally understood to guard the island against negative spiritual forces from the sea. Balinese tradition credits its founding to the 11th-century sage Empu Kuturan, with the site's spiritual stature substantially built up centuries later by the itinerant priest Dang Hyang Nirartha, who is also linked to other major Balinese sea temples such as Tanah Lot. Uluwatu is unusual among Bali's sea temples for also being classified as a directional temple, giving it a dual status in Balinese sacred geography.

The temple honors the divine in its manifestation as Rudra, a destructive and transformative aspect associated with dissolution and renewal, reflecting Uluwatu's role at the meeting point of land, sea, and sky. Alongside its religious function, the site is known for its resident troop of long-tailed macaques, regarded locally as temple guardians (and notorious among visitors for snatching loose belongings), and for the nightly Kecak fire dance performed on an open-air stage overlooking the ocean, which dramatizes an episode of the Ramayana against the backdrop of the sunset. As at other Balinese temples, ritual life follows periodic odalan (anniversary) ceremonies tied to the 210-day pawukon calendar, and Uluwatu remains an active site of Hindu worship alongside its role as one of Bali's most-visited cultural landmarks.
