Basara sits on the banks of the Godavari in Nirmal district and is one of only two major temples on the Indian subcontinent dedicated to Saraswati, goddess of knowledge and learning — the other being Sharada Peeth, now across the border in Pakistan-administered Kashmir. Local tradition explains the town's name as a corruption of "Vasara," recalling a legend that the sage Vyasa, traditional compiler of the Mahabharata, retreated here with Vishwamitra after the Kurukshetra war in search of a tranquil place to meditate, and it is this association that devotees credit with the site's original sanctity. Popular accounts also attribute the founding of the original shrine to a 6th-century ruler remembered as Bijjala of Nandagiri, in present-day Karnataka, though this specific claim rests on thin sourcing and its precise date and the ruler's identity remain difficult to confirm independently. What later sources agree on is that the temple's images were disturbed during medieval-era invasions and reinstalled around the 17th century.

Alongside the principal Saraswati murti — worshipped here in her fierce, all-knowing "Gnana" (wisdom) aspect rather than the gentler form seen elsewhere — the temple complex also enshrines Lakshmi and Mahakali, giving Basara something of a Tridevi character. The temple is best known today for its Akshara Abhyasam ceremony, in which young children are ceremonially initiated into learning, writing their first letters in rice grain before the goddess, a rite that draws families from across Telangana and neighboring states, especially around Vasanta Panchami and the Dasara season.
