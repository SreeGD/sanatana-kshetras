Simhachalam's presiding deity is worshipped in a composite form found at few other temples: Varaha Narasimha, combining Vishnu's boar avatar (Varaha, who slew the demon Hiranyaksha) with his man-lion avatar (Narasimha, who slew Hiranyaksha's brother Hiranyakashipu). Temple tradition credits Hiranyakashipu's own son, the devotee Prahlada, with building the very first shrine to this composite form on the hill after his father's death — a legend rather than a documented event, since inscriptions found at the complex only go back to 1087 CE, well before any known written record of who actually raised that earliest structure.

The temple as it stands today is much better documented: it was built by the Eastern Ganga king Narasingha Deva I and formally consecrated by his son Bhanudeva I in 1268 CE, after which it received continued patronage across several centuries and dynasties, including the Cholas, the Reddy kings, the Gajapatis of Odisha, and the Tuluva rulers of the Vijayanagara Empire — a span of royal interest reflected in the nearly five hundred inscriptions recovered from the site, the latest dated as late as 1798 CE. The temple's most distinctive living practice is the Chandanotsavam: for all but a single day each year, the main idol is kept entirely coated in sandalwood paste, and only on Akshaya Tritiya is that paste removed so pilgrims can view the deity's actual form for about twelve hours before it is reapplied.
