On Manikut hill in Hajo, about 30 km west of Guwahati, the Hayagriva Madhava Temple is dedicated to Hayagriva, the horse-headed form of Vishnu, worshipped jointly here with Madhava (also Vishnu). The standing structure was completed in 1583 under the Koch dynasty by Raghudeva Narayan, as recorded in an inscription at the site; its lower walls carry a continuous frieze of carved elephants reminiscent of the rock-cut temples at Ellora, while upper registers depict Vishnu's ten avatars and scenes from the Ramayana and Mahabharata. Inside the sanctum, an oil lamp is kept perpetually burning (akhanda deep), fed through a simple gravity-tube system — a tradition the temple maintains to this day.

Hajo is unusual as a living multi-faith pilgrimage center: alongside the Hindu shrines on Manikut hill (which also include Shaiva and Shakta temples dating from the 10th to 16th centuries), a 17th-century Muslim shrine called Powa Mecca stands on a neighboring hill, and some Buddhist communities of Bhutan and Tibet hold the Hayagriva Madhava temple to be part of their own sacred geography — a belief connected in their tradition to the Buddha's parinirvana (final passing), reinforced by an "Om Mani Padme Hum" inscription found near the Hayagriva image. This Buddhist identification is a matter of specific community tradition rather than independently documented history, but it makes Hajo one of the few sites in India actively venerated across Hindu, Buddhist, and Muslim tradition alike. The temple's own festival calendar centers on Doul Utsav (Holi) and Janmashtami, alongside year-round Vaishnava worship.
