Tucked into a deodar-forested valley in Uttarakhand's Almora district, Jageshwar is one of the most significant Shaiva pilgrimage and heritage sites in the central Himalaya: a dense cluster of roughly 125 ancient stone temples, part of a wider valley complex of more than 200 structures, dated across the 7th to 14th centuries CE. The Archaeological Survey of India protects 151 of the pre-12th-century structures as monuments of national importance, and the site's clustered, small-shrine architecture, unlike the single grand temple more typical elsewhere, is itself part of what makes it distinctive.

Who built Jageshwar, and for whom, remains debated. Colonial-era scholarship attributed the temples to the regional Katyuri or Chand dynasties, but later research has found little firm textual support for that claim; historians now more often point to patronage by ascetics of the Pashupata Shaiva tradition, under whom the valley is thought to have functioned as a major seat of Lakulisha/Pashupata Shaivism, regionally remembered as a "northern Kashi." Devotional literature reinforces this Shaiva prestige: the Dwadasha Jyotirlinga Stotra names Jageshwar alongside the twelve canonical Jyotirlingas, even though it does not appear on most standard lists of the twelve itself.

Today Jageshwar draws pilgrims and heritage visitors alike, with its largest gatherings during the spring Maha Shivaratri Mela and the monsoon-season Jageshwar Monsoon Festival (Shravani Mela) each July/August, when the temple lanes fill with worshippers even as the site's UNESCO World Heritage tentative-list status and ASI protection keep its ancient stonework at the center of Uttarakhand's archaeological heritage.
