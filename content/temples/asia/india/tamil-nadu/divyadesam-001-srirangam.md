The temple of Sri Ranganathaswamy at Srirangam, on a river island between the Kaveri and Kollidam some 12 km north of Tiruchirappalli (Trichy), is revered as the foremost of the 108 Divya Desams and is honoured in Srivaishnava tradition as Bhuloka Vaikuntam, the Vaikuntam upon earth. Here the Lord reposes as Sri Ranganatha, the Moolavar, in the recumbent posture upon the serpent Adisesha, facing south toward Lanka, an attitude tradition associates with his grace shown to the devotee-king of the south. According to the sthala purana, the self-manifest image was worshipped by Brahma and later by the solar dynasty, and was borne to the south by Vibhishana, brother of Ravana, who wished to install it in Lanka; the Lord chose instead to abide forever on the island of Srirangam, granting Vibhishana the boon that he would gaze ever upon him. No other Divya Desam is so abundantly sung in the Naalayira Divya Prabandham: it is the only one of the 108 glorified by every Alwar, drawing 247 pasurams in all, from the first three Mudhal Alwars Poigai, Bhoothath and Pey, through Thirumazhisai Alwar, Nammalwar, Kulasekara Alwar, Periyalwar, Andal, Thondaradippodi Alwar, Thiruppaan Alwar and Thirumangai Alwar. Thiruppaan Alwar, tradition holds, sang his Amalanaadhipiraan here, beholding the Lord from foot to crown and desiring to see nothing more thereafter, while Thondaradippodi Alwar served the Lord through his flower garden and his Thirumaalai and Thiruppalliyezhuchchi.

Beyond legend, the temple's documented history is substantial. Wikipedia's account traces the earliest structure to the Chola ruler Dharmavarma, rebuilt by the early Chola king Killivalavan, with mandapas near the sanctum dating to roughly 100-300 CE under the Uraiyur Cholas; later Pandya, Hoysala, Vijayanagara, and Maratha-period inscriptions and additions built up the complex over centuries, including 15th-century Vijayanagara gilding of the sanctum's apsidal roof. The temple suffered two major medieval invasions: a 1311 CE raid by the Delhi Sultanate general Malik Kafur, who plundered the golden processional icon, and a far more destructive 1323-1327 CE invasion under Muhammad bin Tughluq, in which temple tradition holds some 13,000 Srivaishnava defenders died defending the shrine; the principal deity icons were secretly moved to safety in Tirunelveli and the Tirupati hills before being reinstalled after the Vijayanagara commander Kumara Kampana's 1371 CE reconquest and reconsecration of the temple. The result is one of the largest functioning Hindu temple complexes in the world in continuous worship: seven concentric prakarams (enclosures) whose walls run over six miles in total, 21 gopuram towers including a 13-tiered, 73-metre rajagopuram only completed in 1987, some 81 shrines and 39 pavilions spread across roughly 63 hectares (155 acres), and over 600 inscriptions in Tamil, Sanskrit, Kannada, Telugu, Marathi, and Odia spanning the 7th/9th to 17th centuries. The temple is on UNESCO's World Heritage tentative list and received UNESCO's 2017 Asia-Pacific Award of Merit for cultural heritage conservation. Srirangam stands as the spiritual capital of the Sri Vaishnava sampradaya, sanctified by the later acharyas, and its grandeur and great festivals continue to draw pilgrims as the very heart of the Divya Desam tradition.

## Festivals

As the foremost of the Divya Desams, Srirangam observes one of the richest festival calendars in the Srivaishnava tradition. The grandest is the 21-day Vaikunta Ekadasi / Adhyayana Utsavam Brahmotsavam in the Tamil month of Margazhi (December/January), with its Pagal-pathu and Ra-pathu recitations of the Naalayira Divya Prabandham, when the Lord passes through the Paramapada Vasal, the gateway of heaven, before crowds reported at around a million visitors. Serthi Sevai, during Panguni Uthram (March/April), and Jyeshtabhishekam are also confirmed observances; the Float festival (Theppotsavam) on the temple tank, Pavithrotsavam, and the wider Chithirai-season festivals are traditionally kept as well, alongside daily worship rich in temple honours.

## Location & Access

The temple stands on Srirangam island between the Kaveri and Kollidam rivers, about 12 km north of Tiruchirappalli (Trichy) and roughly 325 km southwest of Chennai, in the Chola Nadu region of Tamil Nadu, near coordinates 10.86 N, 78.69 E. Trichy, with its railway junction, bus network, and international airport, is the nearest major transport hub, and the island is reached easily from the city by road. Like most Vishnu temples, it generally observes morning and evening darshan hours with a midday break; exact daily timings were not confirmed in the sources checked and should be verified with the temple (HR&CE) or official sources before visiting.
