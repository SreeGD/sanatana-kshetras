Tenavarum, on Dondra Head at Sri Lanka's southernmost tip, was once the southernmost of the island's five traditional Pancha Ishwaram Shiva shrines and one of the most historically resonant temple complexes in South Asia — a site old enough to have been recorded by the Greco-Roman geographer Ptolemy in 98 CE under the name "Dagana." Its major architectural development, in Pallava-influenced style, took place between the 6th and 8th centuries CE, with a Ganesh shrine established and a Vishnu shrine restored in 790 CE under royal patronage. The temple's presiding deity, Tenavarai Nayanar, was worshipped as a form encompassing both Shiva and Vishnu, and locally as Upulvan by Sinhalese devotees — an unusually syncretic identity among Sri Lanka's major Hindu shrines, and the temple was also counted among the 275 Paadal Petra Sthalams glorified in early Tamil Tevaram hymns.

Portuguese forces destroyed the temple in February 1588, leveling the complex and later building a Catholic church over part of the site — a fate shared with several of Sri Lanka's other historic Hindu shrines during the colonial period. Unlike its sister Pancha Ishwaram temples, which were all rebuilt as active shrines in the 19th and 20th centuries, Tenavaram's site today is documented in the sources reviewed for this entry primarily through archaeology: 20th-century excavations recovered a Shiva lingam, a Nandi statue, and a Ganesh image, and a small reconstructed stone structure known as the Galge was repaired in 1947, while later Sinhalese Buddhist structures were built over parts of the original temple grounds. It is recorded here as a heritage site rather than an actively worshipped temple on that basis, though the possibility of ongoing small-scale devotion at the ruins was not ruled out by the sources consulted.
