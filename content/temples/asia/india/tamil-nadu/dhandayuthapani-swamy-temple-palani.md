Palani's hilltop shrine is one of the oldest and most visited of the six traditional abodes (Arupadaiveedu) of Murugan, with roots that literary tradition traces to the Sangam age: the devotional poem Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai names the site (under the older names Thiruaavinankudi and Pothini) among Murugan's sacred hills, and Wikipedia's account attributes an early structure to the Cheras in roughly the 2nd-5th century CE, though as with much early Tamil temple history the precise dating rests on limited evidence. Later Pandya-era inscriptions record royal grants to the temple in the 13th century. Architecturally the hilltop sanctum carries an early Dravidian gold-plated tower, and the principal image, worshipped as Dhandapani ("bearer of the staff"), is traditionally said to be made from an amalgam of nine medicinal substances rather than conventional stone or metal.

The temple's own sthala purana is the renunciation legend at the heart of the Arupadaiveedu tradition: when Ganesha won a contest over a divine fruit by simply circling their parents Shiva and Parvati rather than circling the world as Murugan had set out to do, Murugan felt cheated, shed his princely trappings, and withdrew to this hill as a wandering ascetic, worshipped here in that stripped-down, staff-bearing form rather than as a prince or warrior. A related episode tells of the asura Idumban, sent by Sage Agastya to carry two hills southward, who was defeated by Murugan in combat near here — with one of the two hills said to remain as Palani hill itself. A separate shrine at the foot of the hill honors Murugan in his youthful form as Bala Murugan (Kulanthai Velayuthaswami), so that pilgrims encounter both the child-prince and the renunciate ascetic within the same temple complex.
