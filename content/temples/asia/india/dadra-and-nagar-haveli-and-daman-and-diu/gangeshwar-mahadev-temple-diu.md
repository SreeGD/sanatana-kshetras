Diu's Gangeshwar Mahadev Temple is an unusual sea-level cave shrine set directly among rocks on the Arabian Sea shore near Fudam village, a few kilometers from Diu town. Its distinguishing feature is a set of five Shiva lingas of differing sizes positioned so that they are submerged by the tide and only become visible and accessible to pilgrims at low water — a natural rhythm that governs when the temple can actually be visited. Within the cave, pilgrims also encounter subsidiary images of Ganesha, Vishnu and Lakshmi. The name Gangeshwar, "Lord of the Ganga," refers to Shiva's traditional role as the one who received the descending river Ganga in his matted hair.

Local tradition credits the temple's origin to the Pandava brothers of the Mahabharata, said to have fashioned and worshipped the five lingas here during their years of anonymous exile, and this legend underlies a popular claim that the site is some 5,000 years old. That figure, however, has no archaeological or historical corroboration, and no documented founding date for the shrine could be verified from available sources — this entry treats the Pandava association explicitly as oral tradition rather than established history. What is well attested is the temple's continued active use as a tide-governed pilgrimage site in a union territory, formed by the 2020 merger of Daman and Diu with Dadra and Nagar Haveli, where Hindu, Portuguese-colonial Catholic, and Jain heritage sit close together.
