Muktinath, high in Nepal's Mustang District near the Thorong La pass at roughly 3,800 meters, is one of the most unusual shrines among the 108 Divya Desams: a remote Himalayan site sacred simultaneously to Vaishnava Hindus and Tibetan Buddhists. The presiding deity, worshipped here as Mukti Narayanar ("Lord of liberation"), is counted among the eight self-manifest (Svayam Vyakta) forms of Vishnu, and the temple complex is known in Hindu tradition as Mukti Kshetra, "the place of liberation." Its sanctity is bound up with the shaligrama stones found in the nearby Kali Gandaki riverbed, naturally occurring ammonite fossils that Srivaishnava tradition reveres as self-manifest forms of Vishnu, and the site's glorification appears in the Vishnu Purana's Gandaki Mahatmya as well as in hymns by the Alvars Periyalwar, Thirumangai Alwar, and Kulasekhara Alwar preserved in the Naalayira Divya Prabandham.

The temple complex is physically distinctive for its 108 carved stone waterspouts, or mukti dharas, through which pilgrims traditionally bathe in sequence as an act of purification, and for the adjoining Jwala Mai shrine, where a naturally occurring flame fed by underground gas burns beside a spring — traditionally read as uniting fire and water at a single site. Tibetan Buddhists venerate the same location as Chumig Gyatsa, "Hundred Waters," one of the twenty-four sacred Tantric places, honoring the temple's murti as a manifestation of the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara; local tradition also credits Padmasambhava with crafting a self-portrait statue here before continuing on to Tibet, now kept at a nearby monastery. No firmly documented founding date or builder survives for the temple, but its place in the Alvars' hymns places it among the most anciently attested of the Divya Desams beyond the Tamil country, and it remains today a major, actively visited pilgrimage site reached via the Annapurna Circuit trekking route or by short flights and jeep roads into Mustang.
