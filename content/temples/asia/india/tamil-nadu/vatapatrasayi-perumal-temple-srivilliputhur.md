Srivilliputhur is revered above all as the birthplace of Andal (Kodhai), the only woman among the twelve Alvars and one of the most beloved figures of the Sri Vaishnava tradition. According to temple legend, the sage-poet Periyalvar (originally the temple garland-maker Vishnuchittar) found an infant girl lying beneath a tulasi plant in this very temple's garden; he raised her as his own daughter, and she grew to compose the Thiruppavai and the Nachiyar Thirumozhi, hymns of intense devotion to Vishnu that remain central to Sri Vaishnava worship to this day. Tradition holds that Andal was ultimately united with Ranganatha of Srirangam, and the temple here enshrines a bridegroom form of the deity, Rangamannar, alongside the temple's own presiding form, Vatapatrasayi.

The name Vatapatrasayi - "he who reclines on a banyan leaf" - is explained by the temple's sthala purana: the sages Bhrigu and Markandeya are said to have performed penance in this forest tract, then known as Champaka or Varaha Kshetra, when they were troubled by a demon named Kalanerai; Vishnu appeared reclining upon a tender banyan leaf to vanquish the demon and protect the sages, and has been worshipped in that reclining form ever since. Both Andal and her father Periyalvar sang extensively of this shrine, giving it a scriptural stature within the Naalayira Divya Prabandham to match its status as one of the 108 Divya Desams. Architecturally and historically, the temple carries inscriptions spanning the Chola, Pandya, and Vijayanagara Nayak periods, from roughly the 10th to the 16th centuries CE, with further embellishment under later rulers such as Thirumalai Nayak and Rani Mangammal. Its soaring rajagopuram, rising some 192 feet, is among the tallest temple towers in India and has been adopted as the official emblem of the Government of Tamil Nadu - a rare instance of a Divya Desam's architecture entering the state's civic iconography.
