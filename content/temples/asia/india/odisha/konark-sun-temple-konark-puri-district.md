Built around 1250 CE by King Narasimhadeva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty — the same lineage responsible for the Jagannath Temple at Puri — the Konark Sun Temple was conceived as a colossal stone chariot for the sun god Surya, its base ringed by 24 elaborately carved wheels roughly 3.7 meters across and drawn, in stone, by seven horses. European sailors once called it the "Black Pagoda" for its dark, weathered stone, distinguishing it from the lime-washed "White Pagoda" at Puri, and it served for centuries as a landmark for ships navigating the Bay of Bengal. Konark exemplifies Kalinga temple architecture at its most ambitious — its original tower is thought to have once risen past 60 meters before collapsing — and the pairing of its wheels is traditionally read as symbolizing the months of the Hindu calendar.

Much of the temple has stood in ruins for centuries; the exact cause remains disputed among historians, with theories ranging from structural failure to deliberate damage during later invasions, and many of its original sculptures were removed, including pieces taken to the British Museum during the colonial period. Today the sanctum is without its roof and without an intact, actively worshipped image, and the site is maintained by the Archaeological Survey of India as a protected monument — inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984 — rather than functioning as a temple with daily ritual worship. Its pilgrimage character survives chiefly through the annual Chandrabhaga Mela, held on the nearby beach around February, when devotees gather for a ritual dawn bath associated with sun worship.
