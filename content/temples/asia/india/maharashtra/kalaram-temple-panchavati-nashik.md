Panchavati - the leafy neighbourhood of Nashik on the northern bank of the Godavari river that gives the Kalaram Temple its setting - is identified in tradition as the place where Rama, Sita, and Lakshmana lived for a stretch of their fourteen-year forest exile, and as the spot where Lakshmana is said to have cut off the nose of the demoness Shurpanakha, the episode that sets the Ramayana's war in Lanka in motion. The temple's own name comes from the black ("kala") stone image of Rama enshrined at its heart, flanked by Sita and Lakshmana, with a black Hanuman idol placed at the entrance so that devotees can see the central deity from the doorway.

While popular tradition claims an idol more than two thousand years old and traces the original shrine to the Rashtrakuta period (7th-11th centuries), Wikipedia flags this antiquity as unverified. What is documented is the temple's reconstruction around 1700, funded by the patron Sardar Rangarao Odhekar after the black Rama idol was reportedly recovered from the Godavari; the rebuilt structure's 14 steps are said to symbolise Rama's fourteen years of exile, and its mandapa is supported by 84 pillars.

Kalaram Temple's significance extends well beyond its epic associations: it was the site of the Kalaram Mandir Satyagraha, a landmark anti-untouchability campaign led by B. R. Ambedkar that began on 2 March 1930, saw a major confrontation on Rama Navami that same year (9 April), and continued until 1935 in the fight to open the temple's doors to Dalit worshippers. Today the temple remains an active and popular pilgrimage site within Nashik, with Rama Navami, Navaratri, and Diwali its principal annual observances.
