San Phra Kan, at the center of a busy roundabout in Lop Buri, central Thailand, is a syncretic Hindu-Buddhist shrine famous both for its ancient religious images and, more recently, for the large population of long-tailed macaques that have made the site their home, earning it the popular nickname "Monkey Temple." The shrine houses two principal images: a reclining Vishnu (Narayana) figure known as Thap Lang Narai Banthomsin, and a separate, older Khmer-period stone image, Chao Pho Phra Kan, which sources describe as likely representing either Vishnu or the Buddhist bodhisattva Avalokitesvara — devotees today gild this image with gold leaf as an act of devotion.

The shrine's base may date to around 1100 CE from the Khmer period, though the French archaeologist Jean Boisselier suggested it may originally have served as a Buddhist stupa base rather than a Hindu shrine. A brick-and-stucco shrine in Thai-Persian architectural style was added during the reign of King Narai in the 17th century, when Lop Buri served as a secondary royal capital, and the structure seen today was rebuilt between 1951 and 1952, funded by local civil servants together with Prime Minister Plaek Phibunsongkhram. San Phra Kan remains an active devotional site and a major Lop Buri tourist attraction, reflecting the same blend of Hindu iconography and Thai folk-Buddhist practice found at other prominent shrines in this directory.
