Alopi Devi Mandir, in the Alopibagh locality near Prayagraj's Sangam — the confluence of the Ganges, Yamuna, and the mythical underground Saraswati — is unusual among the temples in this directory for having no idol at all: the object of worship is a wooden doli, a bridal palanquin, kept under worship in place of any carved image. Two distinct origin stories circulate for the site, and sources do not agree on which is the more historically grounded. One account places Alopi Devi among the Shakti Peethas, holding that a fragment of Sati's body (fingers, in one listing) fell here when her corpse was cut apart; but this claim is itself contested, since Prayagraj's more widely and consistently recognized Shakti Peetha is the separate Lalita Devi Temple elsewhere in the city, not covered by this entry. The second and seemingly more locally rooted legend tells of a bride whose wedding procession was attacked by robbers in the forest that once stood here; she vanished without a trace ("alop" meaning "disappeared"), leaving only her palanquin behind, which locals began to venerate as the vanished goddess, Alopi Devi.

No confirmed founding date survives for the temple itself. The wider Sangam area is documented as having been developed under the Maratha chief Mahadji Shinde around 1771-72, with further renovation by Maharani Baiza Bai in the early-to-mid 1800s, but neither record specifically dates the Alopi Devi shrine's own construction. Its modern prominence as a pilgrimage stop is described as having grown substantially since the 1990s, and it remains a popular, if historically thinly documented, stop for pilgrims visiting the Sangam today.
