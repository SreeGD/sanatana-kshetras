The Puthia temple complex, east of Rajshahi city, is one of the largest surviving clusters of Hindu temple architecture in Bangladesh, built up over more than two centuries by the Puthia zamindari family. The estate's origins are traced to a Mughal-era land grant in the 16th century, traditionally associated with a figure named Bhatsacharya; his descendant Nilambar was later granted the title of Raja by the Mughal emperor Jahangir, and under his line the Puthia estate grew into the second-largest, and reputedly wealthiest, zamindari in British Bengal — wealth that financed an unusually dense concentration of temple building on the family's home ground.

The complex's centerpiece is the Bhubaneshwar Shiva Temple, built in 1823 and often described as the largest Shiva temple in Bangladesh, housing one of the country's largest black basalt Shiva lingas; it was damaged during the 1971 Liberation War and is now a protected monument. Nearby stands the Pancha Ratna Govinda Temple, built in the mid-19th century by a queen of the Puthia family and covered in fine terracotta panels depicting the Radha-Krishna devotional cycle, alongside a separate Jagannath Temple and several smaller shrines, including the Chota and Bara Anhik mandirs and a smaller Govinda temple. The Puthia family itself left for India after Bengal's zamindari system was abolished in 1950, and while some of the complex's structures continue to see active worship, conservationists have flagged ongoing threats from nearby development and limited restoration funding to the site's long-term survival.
