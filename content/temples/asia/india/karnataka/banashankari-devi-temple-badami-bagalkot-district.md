Near Badami, the former Chalukya capital renowned for its rock-cut cave temples, stands the shrine of Banashankari — "goddess of the forest" — worshipped here as Shakambhari, an incarnation of Parvati associated with sustaining the earth through vegetation and grain. The black-stone image at the heart of the temple shows the goddess seated on a lion, her many arms bearing ritual implements, trampling a demon underfoot, an iconography shared with the broader Shakambhari cult found across the Deccan. The site's sanctity dates to the Chalukya period, with tradition holding that the image was installed in the early seventh century under Chalukya king Jagadekamalla I, making Banashankari one of the oldest continuously worshipped goddess sites associated with the Badami Chalukyas, alongside the dynasty's better-known cave temples a few kilometers away.

The temple as pilgrims encounter it today, however, is substantially an eighteenth-century reconstruction: the Maratha chieftain Parushuram Agale rebuilt the shrine in 1750 in Vijayanagara architectural style, after the original structure had suffered damage and neglect during the intervening centuries of political upheaval in the region. The temple's largest annual event, the Banashankari Jatre, begins on the full moon of the Hindu month of Pushya (January-February) and runs for roughly three weeks, combining a chariot procession, a boat festival on the temple's sacred tank, and a Palleda Habba produce fair that reflects the goddess's association with agricultural abundance — drawing pilgrims from across Karnataka and neighboring Maharashtra.
