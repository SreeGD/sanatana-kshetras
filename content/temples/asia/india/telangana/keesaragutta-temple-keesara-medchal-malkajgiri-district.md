On a hillock at Keesara, roughly thirty kilometers from Hyderabad, the Keesaragutta Temple combines a popular Ramayana-linked legend with some of the oldest archaeological evidence of religious life in the Telangana region. Local tradition holds that Rama, after killing the demon king Ravana, came to this hill to perform penance and installed a Shivalinga here to atone for the sin of killing a Brahmin — Ravana being learned in the Vedas despite his demonic nature — and that the linga is regarded as self-manifested (swayambhu): when Hanuman, sent to fetch a linga from Varanasi for the consecration, arrived too late, Shiva had already appeared of his own accord, and the Varanasi linga was installed as a secondary image rather than displacing the one already present.

Beyond the legend, the hill has yielded genuine archaeological finds pointing to a much longer, layered religious history: an early Telugu inscription dated to around the fifth century CE, among the earliest such inscriptions known from Telangana, along with twelve Jain Tirthankara images from the fourth or fifth century CE unearthed at the site in 2014 and architectural fragments attributed to the Chalukya period — evidence that Jain, Buddhist, and Hindu worship likely coexisted on or near this hill well before the temple's present Shaiva identity solidified. Today the temple functions as an active Shiva shrine in Dravidian style, with Maha Shivaratri drawing several hundred thousand pilgrims from greater Hyderabad and beyond.
