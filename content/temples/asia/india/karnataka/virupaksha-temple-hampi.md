A modest shrine is believed to have stood on this site as far back as the 7th century CE, on the south bank of the Tungabhadra River at what later became the imperial capital of Vijayanagara. Successive rulers added to it over the centuries — inscriptions and structural additions point to patronage under the late Chalukyas and later the Hoysalas — before it was transformed into the sprawling complex seen today under the Vijayanagara Empire, with the chieftain Lakkana Dandesha credited with major work during the reign of King Deva Raya II in the 15th century. Its nine-tiered eastern gopuram, roughly 50 meters tall, dominates the Hampi skyline and remains one of the most recognizable landmarks of the ruined capital.

The temple's deity, Virupaksha, is a form of Shiva closely tied in local tradition to the goddess Pampa, whose name is preserved in "Hampi" itself. Devotional memory holds that this hill and riverbank were the site of Pampa's penance and eventual marriage to Shiva, and that union is still commemorated each December in a wedding festival that draws pilgrims from across the region — a living ritual rather than a purely historical claim.

When the Vijayanagara Empire fell after the 1565 Battle of Talikota and its capital was sacked and largely destroyed, the Virupaksha Temple was among the few structures to survive relatively intact, and worship here is said to have continued without major interruption since. It stands today as the only major temple in Hampi still in active daily use, and forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage-listed Group of Monuments at Hampi, inscribed in 1986.
