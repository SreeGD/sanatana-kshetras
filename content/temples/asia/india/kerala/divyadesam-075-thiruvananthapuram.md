The Anantha Padmanabhan (Sree Padmanabhaswamy) Temple at Thiruvananthapuram is one of the most celebrated of the 108 Divya Desams and, by most measures, the wealthiest and most extensively documented temple in this directory's Kerala coverage. The presiding deity is worshipped as Anantha Padmanabha, reclining in yoganidra upon Ananta, the thousand-hooded serpent Adisesha, right hand resting upon a Shiva lingam, flanked by his consorts Lakshmi and Bhudevi, with Brahma emerging from a lotus at his navel — the very form and name that gave the city of Thiruvananthapuram its own. The reclining image itself is composed of 12,008 shaligrams (sacred ammonite stones) brought from Nepal's Gandaki river, and is viewed by pilgrims through three separate doorways, each revealing a different aspect of the deity. The temple is documented in Sangam-era Tamil literature, including the Cilappatikaram, and its Divya Desam status rests on hymns composed by Nammalwar in the 7th-8th century CE, among the earliest textually dated of the Kerala shrines in this directory.

The temple's known architectural history centers on the 18th century: Maharaja Marthanda Varma of Travancore undertook major renovations culminating in the idol's re-consecration in 1731 CE, and in a defining act of 1750 he ceremonially surrendered the Kingdom of Travancore itself to Padmanabhaswamy, ruling thereafter as the deity's vassal (Padmanabha Dasa) — a relationship the Travancore royal family maintains symbolically to this day. The temple's Pandyan-style gopuram rises seven tiers and about 100 feet, its foundation dating to 1566; other features include the single-stone Ottakkal Mandapam (quarried at Tirumala), a corridor lined with 365.25 carved granite pillars, and an 80-foot flagstaff. Entry is restricted to Hindu devotees observing a prescribed dress code (men in mundu with angavastram, women in sari or similar traditional dress). Since 2011, the temple has also drawn international attention after court-ordered inventories of its sealed vaults revealed treasure of immense value; following prolonged litigation, a July 2020 Supreme Court of India verdict placed secular administration and asset management under a court-appointed committee, while the Travancore royal family retains traditional ritual and spiritual authority.

## Festivals

The temple's two great annual Utsavams — the Alpasi Utsavam (October/November) and Panguni Utsavam (March/April), each running ten days — culminate in the Aarat procession, when the festival deities are carried to the sea at Shankumugham beach for a ceremonial bath. Navaratri is marked by bringing goddess images from the Padmanabhapuram Palace, Suchindram, and Kumarakovil temples to the nearby Kuthira Malika palace, alongside the Swathi Sangeethotsavam classical music festival. Rarest and grandest of all is the Murajapam and Lakshadeepam, held once every six years: a 56-day cycle of continuous Vedic recitation culminating in the "festival of a hundred thousand lamps."

## Location & Access

The temple stands in the Fort area of central Thiruvananthapuram (Trivandrum), Kerala's capital, within the Malai Nadu region, well connected to Thiruvananthapuram Central railway station, the city bus stand, and Thiruvananthapuram International Airport. Exact daily darshan slot timings vary and should be confirmed with the temple administration; the dress code and Hindu-only entry policy are strictly enforced. Verify current darshan timings and travel details with the temple or official sources before visiting.
