Omkareshwar stands on Mandhata island, where the Narmada River splits around a landmass whose outline is popularly said to trace the shape of the sacred syllable Om. It is counted among the twelve Jyotirlingas, the sites where Shiva is held to have manifested as a self-formed column of light, and is closely paired with the Mamleshwar (also called Amaleshwar) temple across the river on the Narmada's southern bank; together the two are sometimes treated as a single Jyotirlinga presence. Two Puranic accounts explain the site's sanctity: one describes the personified Vindhya mountain worshipping Shiva with a lingam shaped from river sand and being granted his lasting presence here, while another credits the devotion of King Mandhata of the Ikshvaku lineage, whose name the island still carries.

The temple's documented history begins more concretely with the Paramara dynasty, who built the shrine in its recognizable form in the 11th century. Like many Narmada and central Indian temples it suffered damage during medieval-era raids associated with Mahmud Ghazni's campaigns, though it was not destroyed outright. Its present structure owes much to an 18th-century rebuilding effort begun under Rani Gautama Bai Holkar and completed by the Maratha queen Ahilyabai Holkar, whose temple-restoration projects across India are well documented; since independence the Archaeological Survey of India has held an administrative role at the site alongside its continuing function as an active place of worship.
