Thillai Nataraja Temple is the primary shrine of the vast Chidambaram temple complex and the Akasha (ether/space) shrine among the five Pancha Bhoota Sthalams, the group of Shiva temples said to represent the five classical elements — a set already partly covered in this directory by Kanchipuram's earth (prithvi) shrine. It is theologically distinctive among major Shiva temples in that its sanctum enshrines no linga: the presiding image is Nataraja, Shiva as the cosmic dancer, shown performing the Ananda Tandava, the "dance of bliss," which Shaiva legend places in the mythical Thillai forest as the means by which Shiva settled a contest with the goddess Kali. Adjoining the dancing image is the Chidambara Rahasyam, a curtained recess that appears empty save for a string of golden bilva leaves — read in Shaiva philosophy as a symbol of formless, all-pervading Brahman rather than a literal void.

This entry covers the Shiva/Nataraja shrine specifically, the temple actually counted among the Pancha Bhoota Sthalams — distinct from the Govindaraja Perumal shrine, a separate Vishnu sanctum tucked within the same sprawling complex and already catalogued elsewhere in this directory as Divya Desam #40 (Thiruchitrakoodam). The two shrines' coexistence at Chidambaram is often cited as a notable case of Shaiva and Vaishnava sanctity sharing one temple precinct.

The surviving structure traces to the early Chola period: the 9th/10th-century king Parantaka Chola I is credited with gilding the sanctum's roof, and later Chola rulers, notably Rajaraja I and Kulottunga I, expanded and embellished the complex considerably. A Pandya-era ruler later raised the towering eastern gopuram, whose relief carvings depict all 108 karanas (dance postures) of the Natya Shastra, and Vijayanagara patrons restored the temple again in the 16th century after Malik Kafur's forces raided it in 1311. The 7th-century Nayanar saints Thirugnana Sambandar, Appar, and Sundarar all sang of Chidambaram in the Tevaram, and the 9th-century saint-poet Manikkavasagar composed much of his Tiruvasagam here, cementing the temple's place at the center of Tamil Shaiva devotional literature.
