High in the Brahmagiri hills of Wayanad, near where Kerala meets Karnataka, Thirunelli Temple occupies a setting unlike almost any other Vishnu shrine in South India: a stone temple standing alone amid forested mountains at nearly 900 meters elevation, ringed by streams and considered so sacred to death rites that it is popularly called the "Kashi of the South" — a place, like Varanasi in the north, where pilgrims come to perform pitru tharpanam, offerings for the peace of departed ancestors, at the adjoining Papanasini stream. Puranic tradition, referenced across the Matsya, Skanda, Narasimha, and Padma Puranas, identifies the site as Sahyamalaka Kshetra, and temple legend holds that Brahma himself discovered a self-manifested image of Vishnu beneath an amla (Indian gooseberry, or "nelli") tree here — the origin of the temple's name, Thirunelli.

Counted traditionally among the 108 Abhimana Kshetrams of Vaishnava devotion, Thirunelli's remoteness — reached only by a winding hill road from Mananthavady, well off Kerala's more heavily traveled temple circuits — has helped preserve both its forest setting and its distinctive character as a pilgrimage site oriented as much toward ancestor-remembrance as toward conventional darshan. The temple remains an active place of Vishnu worship today, its stone architecture and hill-forest surroundings largely unchanged by the tourism that has reshaped many of Kerala's more accessible shrines.
