Set among the boulder-strewn hills of the Erramala range in Andhra Pradesh's Nandyal district (carved from the former Kurnool district), the Yaganti Uma Maheswara Temple was built in the 15th century under the patronage of Harihara Bukka Raya of the Vijayanagara Empire's Sangama dynasty, and today ranks among the region's most distinctive Shaiva pilgrimage sites for its cave shrines, rock architecture, and folk legends. According to sthala purana, the sage Agastya originally intended to build a temple to Venkateswara here, but the toe of the chosen idol broke during installation; Shiva is said to have then appeared to the sage and proposed that a temple to himself, as Uma Maheswara, would be more fitting for the site, granting Agastya's subsequent request.

The temple's most famous feature is its large monolithic Nandi, popularly believed among devotees to be slowly but continuously increasing in size — a belief reinforced by the removal, at some point in the temple's history, of a pillar said to have been in the growing statue's way. Local prophecy holds that the Nandi will come alive at the close of the Kali Yuga. Beyond the main shrine, Yaganti's hillside houses several rock-cut caves: the Agastya cave, reached by around 120 steep steps and housing a Devi image; a cave shrine associated with Venkateswara; and a cave linked to the wandering saint-poet Potuluri Veera Brahmendra Swami, traditionally said to have composed portions of his prophetic Kalagnanam here.

An active pilgrimage site drawing its largest crowds during Maha Shivaratri, Yaganti combines Vijayanagara-era temple architecture with a wider landscape of caves and hermit-associated sites, making it one of Rayalaseema's notable hill-and-cave Shiva shrines alongside better-known sites such as Srisailam and Ahobilam.
