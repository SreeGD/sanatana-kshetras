Kataragama, in southeastern Sri Lanka, is one of the island's most unusual sacred sites: a single temple complex venerated concurrently by Buddhists, Hindus, the indigenous Vedda people, and Muslims, each with their own shrines within its grounds. Its presiding deity — known to Buddhists as the guardian god Kataragama deviyo and to Hindus as Murugan (Skanda/Kartikeya) — reflects a centuries-long convergence of traditions rather than a single clean origin story. The Pali chronicle Mahavamsa (5th century CE) mentions a town called Kajjaragama in the area, but the first clear literary reference to the shrine as a devotional site does not appear until the 15th-century Tamil poems of Arunagirinathar; scholars remain divided on how old the site's sanctity actually is, with some (such as S. Pathmanathan) proposing it began as a guardian shrine within a Buddhist temple complex around the 13th-15th centuries, and others (such as Heinz Bechert) arguing it preserves an older, pre-Buddhist Vedda mode of veneration.

Administration of the complex today is dominated by Buddhist authorities, who oversee the main shrine and the Kiri Vehera stupa, alongside separately Hindu-administered shrines to Devasena and Shiva and a Muslim mosque honoring local holy men — a division of religious authority that has itself been a source of periodic tension since Buddhist institutions expanded their administrative role from the mid-20th century onward. The annual Kataragama Esala Perahera in July/August, featuring fire-walking and ritual body-piercing by devotees fulfilling vows, remains the site's central living observance and continues to draw pilgrims from all the traditions that venerate it.
