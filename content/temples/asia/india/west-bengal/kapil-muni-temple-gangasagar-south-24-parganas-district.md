Gangasagar, at the southernmost tip of Sagar Island where the Ganga meets the Bay of Bengal, is traditionally identified as the site of one of Hindu scripture's best-known cosmological episodes: the sage Kapila's ashram, where the sixty thousand sons of King Sagara were reduced to ash after disturbing his meditation, and where their descendant Bhagiratha's penances later brought the goddess Ganga down from the heavens to purify their remains -- the same descent-of-the-Ganga narrative recounted in the Ramayana's Bala Kanda. The popular Bengali proverb "sab tirtha bar bar, Gangasagar ekbar" ("all other pilgrimages many times, but Gangasagar at least once") captures the site's standing in regional devotional life, even though the specific identification of this island with the scriptural episode is itself a matter of pilgrimage tradition rather than independently documented history.

The temple's own physical history is one of repeated loss and rebuilding rather than unbroken continuity: an earlier Gupta-era shrine here was lost to the Bay of Bengal's coastal erosion, and after the site was cleared and resettled in the early 19th century under a local zamindar, Yaduram, and later placed under the care of Ramanandi sadhus from Ayodhya, a subsequent bamboo structure was destroyed by cyclones around 1960 -- prompting the brick temple built in 1961 (with West Bengal's first chief minister, Bidhan Chandra Roy, among its funders) and the present structure's 1974 rebuild. That precariousness continues today, with cyclones such as Cyclone Yaas in 2021 still threatening the low-lying site; yet none of it has diminished Gangasagar's standing as host to the Gangasagar Mela, the Makar Sankranti gathering that draws lakhs of pilgrims each January to bathe at the river-sea confluence and is regularly counted among India's largest annual pilgrim congregations after the Kumbh Mela.
