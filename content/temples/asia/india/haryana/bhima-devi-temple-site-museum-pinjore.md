The Bhima Devi Temple Site Museum, in Pinjore town in Haryana's Panchkula district, preserves the remains of a large temple complex dated to roughly the eighth to eleventh centuries CE and generally attributed to the Gurjara-Pratihara period -- a dating that archaeological excavation in 1974 is said to have confirmed. Rather than a single shrine, the original complex followed a Panchayatana plan: a central sanctum surrounded by four subsidiary shrines, a layout also seen at contemporaneous temple sites elsewhere in north India. More than a hundred sculptures have survived from the complex, depicting Shiva, Vishnu, Ganesha, Durga, and other deities, and it is this sculptural richness -- including erotic carving comparable to what is found at Khajuraho and Bhubaneswar -- that has earned the site the popular nickname "Khajuraho of North India."

The complex's popular name, "Bhima Devi," suggests a temple centered on a goddess, yet the source consulted for this entry identifies Shiva as the primary deity within the site's multi-shrine layout -- the Archaeological Department's own assessment holds that the main deity "may have been" Shiva. One possible, though unconfirmed, explanation links the name to the Devi Mahatmya's description of the goddess manifesting in an "enormous form" (Bhimarupa); this entry does not treat that link as settled and flags the naming question for further review rather than asserting either reading as final. The temple no longer functions as an active place of worship: its surviving sculptures -- roughly 85 percent of what has been recovered -- are now displayed as an open-air heritage museum, integrated into the grounds of the adjoining Yadavindra Gardens (the former Pinjore Gardens), making the site as much a stop for garden visitors and history-minded travelers as a pilgrimage destination in its own right.
