Manikaran, in Kulu district's Parvati Valley, is best known for hot springs that boil out of the ground at 64-80°C without a trace of sulfur — an oddity that has anchored Hindu, and separately Sikh, pilgrimage traditions at the same spot for centuries. In Hindu tradition, the springs are explained by a legend of Shiva and Parvati passing through the valley: when Parvati lost an earring in the water, the serpent deity Shesha, provoked into helping search for it, sent up a torrent of boiling, gem-bearing water — a myth still cited by pilgrims bathing here today, particularly for the springs' reputed benefit for joint pain and arthritis.

Two Hindu temples stand near the springs: a Ram Chandra temple, and a separate, smaller Shiva shrine. The Ram Chandra temple is attributed to a Raja Jagat Singh, though sources are not consistent enough to fix a confident date — Wikipedia's own account of Manikaran places the construction in the 15th century, while the Raja Jagat Singh most associated with major temple-building in the Kullu valley is generally dated to the 17th century, and this entry does not attempt to adjudicate between the two. What is better documented is damage: the 1905 Kangra earthquake left the temple visibly tilted, a condition it still shows today. This entry describes only the Hindu temples; the well-known Gurdwara Manikaran Sahib nearby, sharing the same hot springs but built around its own Guru Nanak tradition, belongs to a separate faith and separate history.
