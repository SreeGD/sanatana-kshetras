Indreshwar Mahadev, on the southeastern edge of the historic Newar town of Panauti, sits at the confluence of the Rosi and Punyamati rivers, a sangam setting that has long made Panauti itself a place of pilgrimage. Local tradition traces sanctity at the site back to the early Kirata period, and credits a 13th-century Banepa princess, Viramadevi, with establishing an Indrakuta shrine to Indra, the Vedic rain-god, here — a founding referenced in the Gopalarajavamshavali, a 16th-century Nepali royal chronicle. Despite this Indra-linked founding legend, the temple is popularly known as Indreshwar Mahadev ("Indra's Lord," a name for Shiva) and is counted among Nepal's 64 Swayambhuwa Lingas, the self-manifest Shiva lingams venerated across the country, reflecting a layered history in which Indra's patronage and Shiva's presiding lordship are both woven into the site's identity.

A carved stone at the northeastern corner of the temple's platform is traditionally identified with the wife of the sage Gautama, transformed to stone in a Puranic episode involving Indra's seduction and Gautama's curse — one of several devotional narratives attached to the shrine rather than independently documented history. Architecturally, the present structure is dated to 1294 CE, "built over a lingam," and is described as the oldest surviving temple in Nepal and one of the country's largest and tallest pagoda-style temples; it survived the 2015 Gorkha earthquake largely intact and retains distinctive Newar wood-carving on its lower stories. It remains an active center of Shaiva worship today, drawing its largest crowds for Maha Shivaratri and for Panauti's periodic Makar Mela gathering.
