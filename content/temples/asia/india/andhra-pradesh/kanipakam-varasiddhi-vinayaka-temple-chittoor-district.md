At Kanipakam, a village roughly 11 km from Chittoor town in southern Andhra Pradesh, stands one of South India's most visited Ganesha shrines: a temple built around a swayambhu (self-manifested) idol of Vinayaka that sits partly submerged in a well inside the sanctum, its lower half permanently underwater. Temple tradition traces the shrine's origin to the early 11th century, during the period of Chola influence in the region, with substantial later expansion under the Vijayanagara Empire — an inscription dated 1336 CE records patronage from that era, and the temple's gopuram and pillared mandapas reflect the Dravidian architectural idiom typical of Vijayanagara-period construction in Rayalaseema.

According to the temple's founding legend, three brothers — one mute, one deaf, and one blind — were digging a well on the site when their spade struck a buried stone that began to bleed; digging further uncovered the image of Ganesha, and all three brothers are said to have been miraculously cured of their disabilities upon the idol's discovery. The well surrounding the idol has never been known to run dry, reinforcing local reverence for the site's sanctity. A further, widely held belief among devotees holds that the idol itself has continued to grow gradually larger over the years — cited, in popular tradition, by the fact that a silver armor crafted for the deity some decades ago no longer fits.

Kanipakam is renowned as a favored site for oath-taking and dispute resolution, with litigants and disputing parties traditionally swearing truthful testimony before the deity, a practice said to still carry informal weight regionally. Its 21-day annual Brahmotsavam, beginning on Vinayaka Chavithi, draws large crowds, making Kanipakam one of the most prominent Ganesha pilgrimage centers in South India alongside sites further north and west in the Ganesha pilgrimage landscape.
