The Temple du Gol in Saint-Louis, on Réunion's southwestern coast, was commissioned by Gabriel Le Coat de Kervéguen, a wealthy sugar planter and industrialist, in the mid-nineteenth century - most sources give 1852 or 1856 as the construction date, though a smaller, explicitly hedged set of accounts places the building later, between 1905 and 1910, under a descendant, Robert Le Coat de Kerveguen. French Wikipedia describes it as "le plus vieux temple hindou de l'île" - the oldest Hindu temple on Réunion. As with other Réunion temples of this era, it was built to serve the island's growing population of Indian, largely Tamil, indentured laborers recruited to work sugar estates after the abolition of slavery in 1848 left planters facing a severe labor shortage.

Dedicated to Vishnu, the temple is distinguished by its interior painted frescoes depicting scenes from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, a rare survival of narrative temple painting from this period on the island. In recognition of its age and cultural significance, the Temple du Gol was listed in full on France's official inventory of historic monuments on 29 March 1996, and in 2019 it was selected as one of Réunion's emblematic sites for France's national heritage lottery (Loto du patrimoine) restoration program, funding aimed at preserving the building for future generations.
