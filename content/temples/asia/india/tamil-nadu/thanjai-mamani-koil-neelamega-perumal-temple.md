Thanjai Mamani Koil is a singular case among the 108 Divya Desams: rather than one temple, it comprises three physically separate but adjoining shrines on the outskirts of Thanjavur, near the Vennar river — the Neelamega Perumal temple at Vennatrakarai (the principal shrine), the Manikundra Perumal temple at Manikundram, and the Veera Narasimha Perumal temple at Yazhi Nagar, popularly called "Thanjai Yali Koil." All of the Alvars' hymns dedicated to this Divya Desam address the group collectively, making it the only instance among the 108 where a single Divya Desam designation covers more than one physically distinct temple.

According to the Brahmanda Purana as preserved in temple tradition, three demons — remembered as Tanchakan (or Thanjagan), Tantakan, and Kachamukan, each having received boons from Shiva — harassed the sage Parashara and other rishis at this spot in the Treta Yuga. The assembled sages invoked Vishnu, who destroyed each demon in a different form: Tanchakan by his discus, the Sudarshana chakra; Kachamukan in the form of a Yali, a lion-griffin (giving rise to the Yazhi Nagar shrine); and Tantakan as the boar-avatar Varaha. The place name Thanjavur itself is traditionally said to derive from the demon Tanchakan.

The three shrines carry their own presiding deities and consorts — Neelamega Perumal with Sengamalavalli, Manikundra Perumal with Ambujavalli, and Veera Narasimha Perumal with Thanjai Nayagi — united under a common processional form, Sriman Narayanan. The complex reflects continuous architectural and patronage investment across the Medieval Chola, Vijayanagara, and Madurai Nayak periods, though no specific supporting inscription was located in the sources consulted for this entry. Today the temple group is jointly administered by the Tamil Nadu HR&CE Department and the Thanjavur Palace Devasthanam, a legacy royal trust — an unusual administrative arrangement reflecting Thanjavur's history as a former royal capital. The temple's Brahmotsavam is unusually long, spanning three consecutive Tamil months from Panguni through Vaikasi, and includes the Garuda Sevai, in which eighteen regional deities are ceremonially brought together.
