Jambukeswarar Temple at Thiruvanaikaval, on Srirangam island near Tiruchirappalli, is the Jala/Appu (water) shrine among the five Pancha Bhoota Sthalams, the group of temples representing the five classical elements — its linga sits over a natural underground spring said to keep it perpetually moist. Shaiva legend traces the site's founding to two devotees cursed to be reborn as an elephant and a spider, both of whom worshipped a self-formed linga here beneath a jambu (rose-apple) tree, the elephant bathing it daily with river water and the spider weaving a web overhead for shade; when the elephant, mistaking the web for dirt, tore it down and crushed the spider, the spider is said to have been reborn as the Chola king Kochengat Chola, who then built the temple in gratitude. This legend is popularly tied to a 2nd-century CE founding date, but that date itself is flagged as unverified even in the source consulted for this entry, so it is presented here as tradition rather than settled history.

The temple's more firmly documented history begins with Chola-period inscriptions from the 11th and 12th centuries recording royal grants, and a substantial expansion around 1236-37 CE under the Hoysala king Someswara, who is credited with adding several subsidiary shrines and a seven-tiered rajagopuram. The complex is known for its five concentric enclosure walls (the outermost running roughly 1.6 km) and for a sanctum entrance built deliberately low, according to temple tradition, so that not even a small elephant could enter — a detail linked back to the founding legend. The goddess here, Akhilandeshwari, is worshipped without an accompanying image of Shiva in her own shrine, and tradition holds that the philosopher Adi Shankara, visiting the temple, installed a Sri Chakra and a Prasanna Ganapathy image nearby to calm her fierce (ugra) form — a story reflecting devotional memory rather than an independently documented event.
