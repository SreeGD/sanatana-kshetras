Khatu Shyam Temple, in the small town of Khatu in Rajasthan's Sikar district, has become one of North India's most visited modern pilgrimage sites despite (or perhaps because of) its unusual object of worship: not Krishna directly, but Barbarika, the grandson of Bhima and son of Ghatotkacha, worshipped here as Khatu Shyam. The temple's own tradition holds that it was built by Rao Roop Singh Chauhan in 1027 CE after a divine vision, and that the deity's icon was fashioned from a head unearthed at what is now called Shyam Kund — but this dating belongs to devotional legend rather than confirmed history, and the temple's exact founding period is not independently documented.

The legend behind Khatu Shyam is central to the Mahabharata's lesser-known corners: Barbarika, a fierce warrior gifted three infallible arrows, resolved to fight on the side of the weaker army in the great war, which would have meant the Pandavas' defeat. Krishna, testing his resolve, appeared as a Brahmin and asked Barbarika for his head as alms; Barbarika granted the request, and in return Krishna promised that his severed head — set up on a hill to watch the entire battle — would be worshipped in the Kali Yuga under the name Shyam. That promise is why devotees today address him as "Khatu Shyam" or "Sheesh ke Daani" (the giver of the head). The temple's current structure, built largely in white Makrana marble in a traditional Rajasthani style, hosts its largest gathering during the Falgun Mela, when the Nishaan Yatra brings devotees carrying sacred flags on foot from Reengus, about 17 km away, and the town swells with millions of pilgrims over several days.
