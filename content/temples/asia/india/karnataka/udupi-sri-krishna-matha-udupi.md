Udupi Sri Krishna Matha, on India's Karnataka coast, is the foremost temple of the Dvaita (dualist) school of Vedanta, founded in the 13th century by its architect-philosopher Madhvacharya. Matha tradition holds that Madhvacharya recovered the temple's Krishna icon from a ball of gopichandana sacred clay salvaged from a shipwrecked vessel he had helped save near the coast, and that the icon originally faced east before turning to face west — a reorientation devotees attribute to the intensity of devotion shown by the poet-saint Kanakadasa. Uniquely among major Krishna temples, worship here is not conducted through a conventional sanctum doorway: devotees view the deity through a nine-holed silver window called the Navagraha Kindi, itself seen from an outer window, the Kanakana Kindi, said to have begun as an earthquake-caused crack in the shrine wall that the priest Vadiraja Tirtha chose to widen rather than seal, so that Kanakadasa — turned away from formal entry — could still behold Krishna from the road outside.

Since Madhvacharya's time, the matha has been governed by an Ashta Matha system of eight subordinate monasteries founded by his direct disciples, which take turns administering the temple's rituals and affairs in a rotation formalized as the Paryaya festival, held every two years and, by the matha's own reckoning, in continuous practice for roughly five centuries. The temple's Kerala-influenced architecture and its large daily community feeding program, known as Anna Brahma, are also well-documented features of a site that functions simultaneously as an active pilgrimage destination and as a living seat of Madhva Vaishnava scholarship and monastic governance.
