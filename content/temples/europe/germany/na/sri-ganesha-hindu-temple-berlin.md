Berlin's Sri Ganesha Hindu Temple, at the edge of Volkspark Hasenheide in the Neukölln district, is the culmination of roughly two decades of donation-funded construction by the city's Tamil Hindu community. Planning began in 2005, with a first groundbreaking ceremony on 4 November 2007; construction stalled for want of a building permit, restarting with a second groundbreaking in September 2010, and the foundation and supporting pillars were completed in 2012 before the project again slowed. The temple was finally consecrated with a three-day festival in June 2026 (the main consecration ceremony falling on 7 June), bringing to completion what is described as the largest Hindu temple in continental Europe — second within Europe as a whole only to London's BAPS Shri Swaminarayan Mandir in Neasden, already documented elsewhere in this directory.

Built in the South Indian Dravidian architectural style, the temple is dominated by a 17-metre entrance tower (gopuram), richly decorated and clad in gold, whose portal displays 250 hand-painted figures. Its main altar is dedicated to Ganesha, with secondary altars to Shiva, Vishnu, and Durga. Unlike several other Hindu temples documented in Germany, this one predominantly serves Berlin's Indian-descended Hindu community rather than the city's earlier-established Sri Lankan Tamil community; the built structure covers roughly 850 square metres of ground floor on a property of about 5,000 square metres, and construction is reported to have cost approximately €1.1 million, funded entirely through community donations.

The Ganesha temple is Berlin's second Hindu temple. It was built in part to relieve the smaller, mainly Sri Lankan Sri Mayurapathy Murugan Temple in the Britz district (consecrated 2013, already documented elsewhere in this directory) and to give Neukölln's Indian Hindu community its own dedicated place of worship, amid a Berlin Hindu population estimated at roughly 45,000. Its scale and two-decade construction history have made it a notable civic and architectural landmark in the German capital, illustrating the growth of organized Hindu religious life in Germany alongside the Tamil-community temples such as the Sri Kamadchi Ampal Temple in Hamm and Sri Muthumariamman Temple in Hanover, both already documented elsewhere in this directory.
