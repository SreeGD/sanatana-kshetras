Sri Vaikunda Perumal Temple — known in inscriptions by its original name, Thiruparameswara Vinnagaram (Tamil) or Vishnugriha ("house of Vishnu," Sanskrit) — is among the architecturally most significant temples in Kanchipuram, and indeed in the whole of early medieval Tamil Nadu. Built of stone by the Pallava king Nandivarman II Pallavamalla in the 8th century CE (variously dated to around 690 CE or, on stylistic grounds, to the 770s CE), it represents an important early example of a fully structural Dravidian temple built at a time when many contemporary Pallava monuments were still rock-cut. Its most celebrated feature is a unique three-tiered vimana in which Vishnu is enshrined in three distinct postures stacked vertically — seated on the ground floor, reclining on the first, and standing on the second — a design sometimes described in terms of the chakrabja-mandala plan, and unmatched among the other Divya Desams of the region.

The temple's inner prakara walls carry an extraordinary sequence of sculpted narrative panels and inscriptions recounting Pallava dynastic history, including the disputed royal succession that brought Nandivarman II to the throne. Local tradition folds this history into a foundation legend: it holds that the sage Bharadvaja's celestial-born child was entrusted, at Vishnu's own direction (Vishnu having appeared to a Pallava king in the guise of a hunter), to the Pallava royal house, and that this child — later crowned as Paramesvaravarman — built the temple in gratitude. Historical scholarship, drawing on the temple's own inscriptions, instead credits Nandivarman II Pallavamalla, a king said in these very inscriptions to have originally come from Kambujadesa (present-day Cambodia) before being installed on the Kanchipuram throne as a boy of twelve; the temple's narrative reliefs are understood by historians as a form of dynastic legitimation for this unusual succession.

Thiruparameswara Vinnagaram is counted among the 108 Divya Desams on the strength of ten pasurams composed here by Thirumangai Alvar — himself traditionally understood to have been a contemporary of the Pallava court — and preserved in the Naalayira Divya Prabandham. Today maintained by the Archaeological Survey of India as a centrally protected monument, the temple stands as both a living site of Sri Vaishnava worship and one of the most important surviving records of Pallava art, architecture, and dynastic history in Kanchipuram.
