Unakoti, on a hillside about 8 km from Kailashahar in what is now Tripura's Unakoti district, is one of Northeast India's most striking rock-cut religious sites: a hillside covered in bas-relief carvings dominated by a roughly 30-foot central head of Shiva, known locally as Unakotiswara Kal Bhairava, alongside a standing image of Durga on her lion, colossal figures of Ganesha, and three enormous, half-buried Nandi bulls. Stylistic dating places the carvings in the 7th-9th century CE, with patronage credited to the Deva dynasty that ruled Sribhumi in the historical Samatata region, and a later inscription from the 11th-12th century records the visit of a named pilgrim, Sri Jayadeva -- evidence that the site was already an established pilgrimage destination well before the modern era. In December 2022 Unakoti was added to UNESCO's tentative World Heritage Site list, formal recognition of a scale and antiquity of rock-cut Hindu art that has few parallels in the region.

The site's name -- "one less than a crore" -- is explained by two overlapping legends: in one, a craftsman named Kallu Gurjar was set the task of carving a full crore (ten million) images of Shiva and the gods in a single night to earn a place accompanying Shiva and Parvati to Kailash, but finished one image short by dawn and was left behind; in another, more scripture-flavoured account, Shiva was travelling to Kashi with 9,999,999 gods and goddesses who overslept and were cursed to stone for it. Unlike most temples documented in this directory, Unakoti has no single enclosed sanctum or fixed daily darshan schedule -- it is an open hillside of carvings rather than a conventional built shrine -- but it functions as an active and growing pilgrimage site nonetheless, drawing its largest crowds each April for the Ashokashtami Mela.
