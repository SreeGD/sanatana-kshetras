High in the Brahmagiri hills of Kodagu (Coorg) district, close to the Kerala border, Talakaveri is revered across South India as the traditional source of the Kaveri (Cauvery) river — one of the subcontinent's most sacred waterways, worshipped here in its own right as the goddess Kaveramma. A small temple stands beside a spring-fed tank, or kundike, said to mark the spot where the river first rises; alongside Kaveramma, the shrine also honors Agasthiswara, a form of Shiva whose name recalls the sage Agastya, closely linked in Puranic tradition to the Kaveri's origin story. Tradition holds that the spring's waters travel underground before re-emerging further downstream as the visible river, though there is no permanent surface flow connecting the two except during the monsoon — a detail that marks this as a matter of pilgrimage geography and devotion rather than settled hydrology.

The site's largest gathering comes each October at Tula Sankramana, when thousands of pilgrims travel to Talakaveri to witness the "rise of the fountainhead" — a sudden upwelling of water in the tank at a predicted moment, considered especially auspicious for a ritual bath. The present temple was substantially renovated by the Karnataka state government in 2007. Its priesthood traces to a more local piece of history: the Kodagu ruler Lingaraja I, who reigned in the 1780s, is said to have appointed a Brahmin named Venkappayya as priest after a divine dream, founding a hereditary family of temple priests that, per Wikipedia's account, continues in its ninth generation today.
