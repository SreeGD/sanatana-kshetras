One of Kerala's oldest and most revered Shiva temples, Vaikom Mahadeva Temple enshrines a lingam that temple tradition holds to be among the state's most ancient continuously worshipped Shiva images, associated in legend with the Treta Yuga. According to sthala purana, the demon Khara received three lingams directly from Shiva to install in the south; through divine intervention, one remained fixed at this site, where the sage Vyaghrapada maintained its worship until Parashurama, the traditional founder of Kerala's Brahminical temple order, later discovered, consecrated, and built a temple around it, appointing a Brahmin priesthood to conduct its rites. Shiva is worshipped here under the affectionately local name Vaikathappan, "the lord of Vaikom."

Architecturally, the temple is distinguished within Kerala's temple tradition by its oval-shaped sanctum — reportedly unique among the state's temples — entered by devotees via six steps said to symbolically represent the shedding of spiritual impurity before approaching the deity. Set within an eight-acre temple compound, Vaikom Mahadeva Temple commands reverence from both Shaiva and Vaishnava worshippers and remains, alongside Ettumanoor and Kaduthuruthy, one of the "Tetrayugam" group of ancient Shiva temples traditionally associated with Kerala's earliest recorded Shaiva worship. Its Vaikathashtami festival, held annually on Krishna Ashtami of the Malayalam month Vrischikam, commemorates Shiva's manifestation before Vyaghrapada and remains the temple's principal annual celebration.

Beyond its religious history, Vaikom holds a distinct place in modern Indian civil-rights history as the site of the 1924-25 Vaikom Satyagraha, a landmark nonviolent campaign — involving Mahatma Gandhi, Periyar E.V. Ramasamy, and T.K. Madhavan among its leading figures — demanding that the public roads surrounding the temple be opened to Hindus of all castes, not merely privileged ones. The campaign's eventual, partial success in opening those roads is widely regarded as an early landmark in the long struggle against caste-based temple-entry restrictions across India, giving Vaikom Mahadeva Temple significance well beyond its role as an ancient Shaiva pilgrimage site.
