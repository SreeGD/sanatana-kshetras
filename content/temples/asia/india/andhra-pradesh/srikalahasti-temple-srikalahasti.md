Srikalahasti Temple, on the banks of the Swarnamukhi river in Andhra Pradesh, is the Vayu (wind/air) shrine among the five Pancha Bhoota Sthalams, the group of Shiva temples said to embody the five classical elements. Its name is traditionally read as a fusion of "Sri" (spider), "Kala" (snake), and "Hasti" (elephant), commemorating a legend in which a spider, a snake, and an elephant each worshipped the linga here in their own way — the elephant washing it with river water and offering flowers, the spider spinning a protective web of shade over it, and the snake placing a gem upon it — until each ultimately attained liberation. The temple is also closely tied to the story of the hunter-devotee Kannappa Nayanar, who, seeing what he took to be blood flowing from the linga's eye, offered his own eye in devotion and was prepared to tear out the second before Shiva stopped him and granted him grace; this story is central to Kannappa's place among the 63 Nayanars of Tamil Shaiva hagiography, though it belongs to devotional tradition rather than documented history.

A commonly cited 5th-century founding date for the site could not be independently confirmed in the sources checked for this entry, so it is treated here with caution. What is better documented is the temple's medieval building history: the Chola emperor Rajendra Chola I renovated and largely constructed the main structure in the 11th century, with further patronage from Kulottunga Chola I and the Reddy dynasty, before the Vijayanagara emperor Krishnadevaraya added the temple's imposing 120-foot gopuram and hundred-pillared hall in 1516. Srikalahasti is also widely known today as South India's principal site for Rahu-Ketu worship, a ritual believed to counter the astrological effects of these two nodes; unusually, the temple remains open to worship even during solar and lunar eclipses, when many other temples close.
