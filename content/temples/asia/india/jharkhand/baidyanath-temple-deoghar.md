Deoghar's Baidyanath temple is counted among the twelve Jyotirlingas, the sites where Shiva is traditionally said to have manifested as a column of light, and its founding is described through legend rather than a documented construction date. The best-known account, shared with several other Jyotirlingas, holds that Ravana attempted to carry Shiva's lingam to Lanka but was tricked into setting it down at Deoghar, after which it could never be moved again; a separate, more local tradition instead credits a hunter named Baiju (from whom "Baidyanath" is popularly, though not certainly, derived) with first discovering and worshipping the lingam here. Both are recounted in this entry as tradition, since no inscription or excavation record settles the question.

What is better documented is the temple's more recent administrative history: after the British East India Company's power in Bengal and Bihar grew following the 1757 Battle of Plassey, colonial officials became involved in the temple's affairs, and a collector named Keating eventually returned full administrative control to the temple's high priest — an arrangement that evolved into today's Baba Baidyanath Temple Management Board. The temple itself anchors a walled complex of 22 shrines, with the main sanctum's shikhara rising roughly 72 feet (about 22 m). Its best-known living tradition is the Shravani Mela: during the monsoon month of Shravan, pilgrims known as Kanwariyas walk roughly 105 km from the Ganges at Sultanganj (via the Ajgaibinath temple) to Deoghar to pour the sacred water over the lingam, making it one of the largest annual pilgrim gatherings in eastern India.
