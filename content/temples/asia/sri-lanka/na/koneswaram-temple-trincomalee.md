Koneswaram Temple crowns Swami Rock, a dramatic cliff-top promontory in Trincomalee on Sri Lanka's eastern coast, and is traditionally reckoned the foremost of the island's five ancient Pancha Ishwaram Shiva shrines. Its documented literary trail reaches back at least to a reference in the Vayu Purana, generally dated to around the 3rd century CE, though historians are divided on how much earlier a shrine may have stood at this naturally commanding site, with proposed dates for its founding and for a later renovation credited to a figure called Kullakottan spread across more than a thousand years of disagreement. Hindu legend adds a still older layer: the story that King Ravana tried to tear the sacred mountain loose to heal his ailing mother, only for Shiva to make him drop his sword mid-effort, supposedly leaving the cliff's dramatic fissure still shown today as "Ravana's Cleft."

The temple's best-documented chapter is also its most violent: on 14 April 1622, Portuguese forces under Constantino de Sá de Noronha destroyed the shrine and threw its structure from the cliff into the sea below, with a further destructive episode in 1624; the colonial-era Fort Frederick, still standing beside the temple site, was built in part from the temple's own dressed stones. Buried and submerged images from the original temple were only recovered in the twentieth century — some unearthed from a well in 1950, with further underwater ruins explored by Arthur C. Clarke and Mike Wilson in 1956 — and restoration and rebuilding have continued from the 1950s to the present. Today Koneswaram functions again as a major, actively worshipped Shaiva pilgrimage center, drawing pilgrims to its clifftop sanctum, the adjoining Bhadrakali shrine, and the sacred Pavanasam well below.
