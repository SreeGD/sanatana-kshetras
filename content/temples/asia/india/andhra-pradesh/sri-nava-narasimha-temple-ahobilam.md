Ahobilam — "Thiru Singavelkundram" in the Divya Prabandham — is where the Puranas locate Vishnu's fourth avatar, Narasimha, tearing apart the demon-king Hiranyakashipu on the threshold of a pillar to save his devotee-son Prahlada, fulfilling a boon that the demon could be killed neither by man nor beast, neither indoors nor outdoors, neither by day nor night. The name Ahobilam ("Oh, what a cave!") is traditionally explained as the exclamation of the gods on witnessing Narasimha's ferocity. Uniquely among the 108 Divya Desams, this single entry comprises nine distinct shrines — the Nava Narasimha — spread across Lower (Diguva) and Upper (Eguva) Ahobilam in the Nallamala forest, each marking a different form or episode of the Narasimha legend, from the fierce Ugra Narasimha at the cave believed to be the actual site of Hiranyakashipu's slaying, to the gentler Malola Narasimha seated with consort Lakshmi on his lap.

Thirumangai Alvar, the last of the Alvars and the one most associated with far-flung pilgrimage in the Prabandham, sang ten pasurams glorifying this sthala, referring to it as Singavelkundram. Epigraphic evidence — inscriptions dated to the Chalukya king Kirtivarman II (744–755 CE) through to Vijayanagara ruler Venkatapatiraya II (1585–1614 CE) — attests to over eight centuries of continuous royal patronage from the Chalukyas, Kakatiyas, Reddy kings, and above all the Vijayanagara empire, whose emperors regarded Ahobila Narasimha as a family deity. In 1398 CE the theologian-saint Adivan Satakopa Yatindra Mahadesikan founded the Ahobila Matha here, one of the most prominent Sri Vaishnava monastic institutions, which continues to administer aspects of temple life. The complex suffered damage and looting during periods of regional conflict but was subsequently restored and remains an active and popular pilgrimage and trekking destination.
