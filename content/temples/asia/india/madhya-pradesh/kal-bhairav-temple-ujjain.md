On the banks of the Shipra river in Ujjain, a short distance from the city's more famous Mahakaleshwar Jyotirlinga, stands the Kal Bhairav Temple — dedicated to the fierce, guardian form of Shiva regarded as Ujjain's own protector deity and, within Shaiva tradition, as chief of the Ashta Bhairava, the eight Bhairavas. The Skanda Purana's Avanti Khanda names a King Bhadrasen as builder of an earlier temple on this site, a scriptural claim rather than a dated historical record; more solid archaeological evidence comes from artifacts of the Paramara period (9th–13th centuries CE) recovered here, including stone images of Shiva, Parvati, Vishnu, and Ganesha. The temple as it now stands owes its present form chiefly to Maratha-era restoration, traditionally credited to the general Mahadaji Shinde after 1761, and it was historically an important center for the Kapalika and Aghori ascetic traditions, whose tantric practices remain woven into the temple's ritual character.

The temple is best known today for a single, striking practice: the offering of liquor directly to the deity, one of five traditional offerings within tantric worship at the site. Devotees bring bottles of liquor to the temple's priests, who pour it into a vessel and tilt it toward the deity's mouth as an act of worship, then return roughly a third of it to the devotee as blessed prasad. What was long an informal custom was given official structure in 2015, when the state government established licensed liquor counters at the temple specifically to regulate the practice and protect pilgrims from unlicensed vendors — a rare instance of a state formally institutionalizing what remains, in Hindu temple practice generally, a highly unusual form of offering.
