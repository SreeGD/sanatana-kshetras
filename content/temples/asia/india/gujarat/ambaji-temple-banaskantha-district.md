Ambaji is unusual among major Shakti Peetha temples for what is absent from its sanctum: there is no carved image of the goddess at all. Devotees instead face a plain crevice or void in the rear wall of the sanctum, which priests dress periodically in silk, ornaments, and a bejewelled face-mask to represent the goddess in different aspects — most famously seated on her tiger. This aniconic form is often explained locally as a mark of Ambaji's particular sanctity within the network of Shakti Peethas, sites tradition associates with the scattering of the goddess Sati's body after Shiva carried her corpse across the cosmos in grief. Exactly which fragment is said to have landed here varies between the retellings collected during this research — the temple trust's own website identifies it as the heart, though this is not independently corroborated by the encyclopedic source checked here — so no single claim is asserted beyond the site's general standing as one of the 51 Peethas.

Documented history at Ambaji is thinner than the legend suggests: available sources describe worship here as attested from at least the 14th century, without settling on an exact founding date or patron before that. The temple's real prominence today rests less on any single dated structure than on scale of devotion — the annual Bhadrapad Purnima fair draws several million pilgrims within a single week, making Ambaji one of Gujarat's largest pilgrimage gatherings, alongside a steady year-round stream of visitors given its position on the well-trodden route between Mount Abu and Palanpur.
