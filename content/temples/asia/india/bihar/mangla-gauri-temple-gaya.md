Mangla Gauri Temple, on a hill of the same name overlooking Gaya, is a separate and distinct shrine from the Vishnupad Temple already documented elsewhere in this directory — different deity, different hill, and by tradition a very different origin — though both sit within the same city's crowded pilgrimage landscape. Here the goddess is worshipped as Mangala Gauri, "the auspicious fair one," also called Sarvamangala; tradition holds that when Sati's body was cut apart, her breasts fell at this spot, making it one of the Shakti Peethas counted among the eighteen "maha" (great) Shakta pithas of that tradition. The site's sanctity is referenced across several ancient Puranic texts, including the Padma, Vayu, and Agni Puranas, though these references speak to the antiquity of the site's reputation rather than to any dated construction.

The present cave-style temple structure is attributed to roughly the 15th century and, per the source consulted, credited to a builder named Maadho Giri Ji Maharaj, also known as Baba Dandi Swami — though exactly how this relatively specific attribution reconciles with the much older Puranic references to the site is not made clear in the record checked, and is flagged here for further verification. A fresh check of that same source's infobox surfaced yet another unreconciled figure, "Completed 1300 CE," alongside its own 15th-century claim in the main text — an internal inconsistency this entry notes rather than resolves. The temple complex today also houses subsidiary shrines to Kali, Ganesha, Hanuman, and Shiva, and observes Navratri as its principal annual festival.
