Standing at the confluence of the Arabian Sea, the Bay of Bengal, and the Indian Ocean at India's southernmost point, the Bhagavathy Amman Temple (also called the Kumari Amman Temple) is named in the Ramayana, the Mahabharata, and the Sangam-era Purananuru anthology, attesting to a very long history of veneration at this site, though the frequently repeated claim that the temple is about 3,000 years old is a devotional tradition rather than an archaeologically established date, and no founding dynasty or construction period could be confirmed here. The temple is counted among the Shakti Peethas, the network of sites across the subcontinent sacred to the Goddess, though Puranic sources disagree on whether there are 51, 64, or 108 such sites in total, so its place "among the 108" reflects one reckoning rather than universal consensus. The temple stands apart from, and should not be confused with, the Vivekananda Rock Memorial visible just offshore, a 20th-century monument built in 1970 to commemorate Swami Vivekananda's meditation there, which is a secular commemorative structure and not a place of Hindu worship.

Local sthala purana holds that the goddess undertook penance at this spot in preparation for marriage to Shiva, a union that was ultimately averted, in some tellings through a ruse by the sage Narada, so that she remains the eternal virgin (Kanya) goddess and guardian of this coastline, worshipped here in the same form credited in tradition with the slaying of the demon Banasura. A further local legend explains why the temple's eastern, sea-facing gate is traditionally kept shut: light reflecting off the diamond in the goddess's nose-stud was said to have once been mistaken by ships at sea for a lighthouse, leading to wrecks along the shore. These narrative details are widely recounted in Tamil Shakta devotional tradition but were not located in the specific sources checked for this entry, and are presented here as oral tradition rather than confirmed historical fact.
