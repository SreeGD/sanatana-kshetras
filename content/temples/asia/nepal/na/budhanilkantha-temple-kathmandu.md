Budhanilkantha Temple, at the northern edge of the Kathmandu Valley beneath Shivapuri Hill, is built around one of Nepal's most striking works of religious art: a colossal black basalt image of Vishnu reclining on the coils of the serpent Ananta, submerged to the shoulders in a stone water tank, holding his characteristic chakra, mace, conch, and gem. At roughly five meters long, it is generally regarded as the largest stone carving in the country, and its age is usually estimated at over 1,400 years on stylistic grounds, placing its creation in or around Nepal's Licchavi period — though no firmly dated inscription fixes the year. Local tradition holds that the statue was originally discovered by farmers while plowing a field, a legend rather than a documented event, and it has since become one of the principal sites of Vaishnava worship in the valley alongside Changu Narayan.

The temple carries an unusual royal taboo: since a prophetic warning reportedly given to King Pratap Malla in the seventeenth century that a visit would bring premature death, Nepali monarchs are said to have avoided the site entirely, a practice tradition holds continued for the rest of the monarchy's history. Despite this royal absence, Budhanilkantha has remained a major popular pilgrimage destination, drawing both Hindu and Buddhist worshippers, with the crowds at their largest during Haribodhini Ekadashi, the festival marking Vishnu's mythological awakening from his four-month yogic sleep.
