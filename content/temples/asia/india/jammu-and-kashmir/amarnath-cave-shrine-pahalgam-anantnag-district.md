High in the Himalayas of Jammu and Kashmir, the Amarnath cave shrine centers on a naturally occurring ice stalagmite, formed by freezing water droplets inside a roughly 40-meter-tall cave, that Shaiva tradition reveres as a self-manifest (swayambhu) lingam of Shiva. The ice formation waxes as Himalayan snowmelt feeds it from May through August and gradually recedes afterward — a natural cycle scientists attribute to seasonal temperature change, the cave's water pathways, and even heat from visiting pilgrims, rather than to any fixed, unchanging form.

Tradition holds that the sage Bhrigu was the first to discover the cave, after the sage Kashyapa drained the ancient waters of the Kashmir Valley, and that Shiva himself revealed the site's inner meaning by progressively shedding his worldly attributes along the pilgrimage route on his way to relate the secret of immortality to Parvati: leaving his mount Nandi at Pahalgam, his crescent moon at Chandanwari, his serpent at Sheshnag Lake, and his son Ganesha at Mahagunas Parvat, before finally entering the cave itself.

The modern Amarnath Yatra draws hundreds of thousands of pilgrims each summer — from roughly 12,000-30,000 annually in 1989 to a peak of over 600,000 in 2011, with recent years typically in the 285,000-345,000 range — along two routes, a longer trek from Pahalgam and a shorter, steeper one from Baltal. Given the site's remote, high-security location near the Kashmir Line of Control, the Yatra has been the target of several attacks over the decades (notably in 2000, 2001, 2002, and 2017), and today sees a substantial deployment of central and state security forces each pilgrimage season alongside the usual logistics of altitude, weather, and crowd management.
