Guruvayur's own sthala purana, recorded in the Narada Purana's Gurupavanapura Mahatmya, holds that the temple's Krishna image was originally fashioned by Vishnu himself in Vaikuntha and passed down through Brahma to the sage-king Sutapas and his wife Prishni, eventually reaching Krishna's own era on earth. After Krishna's departure from the world and the submergence of his city Dwaraka, his disciple Uddhava is said to have rescued the image and entrusted it to Brihaspati, guru of the gods, and Vayu, the wind-god, who together carried it to its present site and installed it — the town's name is traditionally read as a fusion of "Guru" and "Vayu" in their honor.

The temple has a well-attested record of surviving repeated turmoil over the centuries. A Dutch raid damaged its western gopuram in 1716 (rebuilt by 1747), Hyder Ali's forces extracted a ransom during an invasion in 1766, and when Tipu Sultan's army advanced on the region in 1789 the temple authorities are recorded to have moved the main deity to Ambalapuzha for safekeeping, only reinstalling it at Guruvayur on September 17, 1792. A catastrophic fire in 1970 destroyed surrounding structures but spared the sanctum itself. The temple was also a focal point of the wider movement against caste-based exclusion in Kerala: the Guruvayur Satyagraha, a campaign running from 1931 to 1947, sought temple-entry rights for all Hindus regardless of caste, and Guruvayur was formally opened to all Hindu worshippers on June 2, 1947.

Guruvayurappan, the four-armed Krishna image at the heart of the shrine, is carved from shaligram stone and holds the conch, discus, mace, and lotus. The temple remains one of Kerala's most visited Krishna shrines, its calendar built around festivals such as Krishna Janmashtami, the eleven-day Guruvayur Ekadasi, and the ten-day Kumbham Utsavam, and its adjoining Punnathurkotta sanctuary is known for housing elephants donated by devotees.
